Macroeconomic Risks from Maritime Trade Disruptions

🌐 Source: Arxiv • 📅 26 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 1 min read (288 words, 📖 Standard)

Recent findings suggest that arXiv:2607.09951v3 Announce Type: replace Abstract: This paper develops a model of maritime chokepoint closures in which interrupting a shipping passage produces losses that are not measured, or even bounded, by the value of the trade that transits it. The losses stem from disruptions to the flow of intermediate inputs that are complementary in downstream production. They travel through the world trade network, which itself rewires around the closed passage. Re-matching displaced trade on the buyer and seller sides of the market, which the model carries out as rationing at fixed prices, limits the damage but does not eliminate it. Across countries, the incidence of these losses is heavy-tailed, and it reaches economies that sell nothing through the passage, some of which buy little through it either. The two ends of a severed corridor lose unequally. At the typical corridor the economies that mainly sell through it lose more than twice as much as those that mainly buy through it, and at the largest energy gate between four and five times as much, as economic geography funnels commodity-concentrated sellers through a single passage while their buyers re-source. Economic geography decides how the loss from closing several chokepoints together compares with the sum of the losses from closing each alone. A shipment stopped at the first of two gates in series cannot be stopped again at the second, so such gates duplicate one another, whereas gates in parallel take different cargo for the most part, so their losses add. Which is why the Middle East scenario costs about a tenth less than the sum of its single closures, while the East Asian and Russia-Europe scenarios cost that sum to within three percent.
