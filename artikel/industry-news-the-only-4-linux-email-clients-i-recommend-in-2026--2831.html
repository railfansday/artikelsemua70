Recent findings suggest that I’ve used Geary as my default email client for a long time because it was simple and had a UI that didn’t get in the way of using the app. However, over the past year or so, I’ve found it has become less and less reliable; it randomly crashes or doesn’t download all of my emails. On top of that, it’s been a while since any fresh features were added. To that end, I’ve spent a lot of time testing email clients for Linux, and these are the only ones I recommend in late 2026.

Thunderbird has been the go-to email client for Linux customers for decades. Thunderbird has all the features you need in an email client: email, calendar, contacts, chat, and tasks. All of this is wrapped up in a UI that’s easy to navigate and even lets you customize the layout for message lists, card view, sorting and threading, and accent color. You can also select from light, dark, or system themes and hide the sidebar.

Also: Why Nextcloud Office is my latest favorite free productivity suite.

Industry observers note that thunderbird allows you to connect IMAP/POP, Exchange, Gmail, and more. It also offers powerful sorting and filtering, so you can promptly find what you’re looking for, even when your inbox is stuffed with email.

In a recent development, more importantly, however, Thunderbird is as stable as it gets. Over the decades, Thunderbird has finally managed to get the feature set, UI, and customizations just right, so it’ll serve nearly any user type. And thanks to extensions, you can also integrate AI into Thunderbird. There are extensions for ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, and Ollama. I was even able to integrate my locally installed Ollama instance to Thunderbird, which is a huge plus.

Thunderbird is free, open-source, and can be installed on any Linux distribution.

According to recent reports, betterbird is considered a “soft fork” of Thunderbird. That’s a fork that stays closely aligned with the original codebase. Betterbird takes Thunderbird and adds or refines several features. For example, Betterbird adds a native Outlook-style multi-line inbox view, vertical tabs, system tray tooltips, and more efficient attachment of recent files.

Industry observers note that betterbird focuses on patching regressions and tracking complications that would otherwise take longer with Thunderbird. Betterbird also does not send any telemetry (such as crash reports or usage data), which many Linux users are keen on. Other ways Betterbird improves Thunderbird include account colors, header pane resizing, secondary sort indicators, more window layouts, reverse threading, complex search terms, boolean filters, Linux system tray support, and a top menu bar.

Also: This lightweight distro revives old PCs destined for the junk pile – and it’s beautiful.

For me, one of the main reasons I choose Betterbird over Thunderbird is the vertical tabs option. I switch to vertical tabs in every app that offers it, and Betterbird does a great job with the feature. You can even open an email in its own vertical tab. Bonus.

My only gripe with Betterbird (and Thunderbird as well) is that they don’t always honor system themes. I spend a good deal of time tweaking my desktop, and prefer that all of my apps follow my custom themes.

Like Thunderbird, Betterbird is free and open-source.

It has been revealed that penguin Mail was one of those email clients that snuck up on me. This email app is available for both Linux and MacOS, and it really shines on Linux. The first thing that struck me was the clean UI, which is perfectly laid out. Unlike Geary, you don’t need to have Penguin Mail maximized to use the three-column interface. What drew me in next was the ability to use local AI with Penguin Mail. I connected the app to my local instance of Ollama, and it worked to perfection; no extensions to install, and no external APIs required.

Sources indicate that penguin Mail supports Gmail, Outlook 365, iCloud, Fastmail, Yahoo Mail, IMAP, and POP. Its layout is also immediately familiar and accessible. As far as services, Penguin Mail includes email and calendar.

Also: I tested Dia browser – it beats Chrome’s built-in Gemini tool in one big way.

Recent findings suggest that but Penguin Mail is a bit picky about how it allows you to set up Gmail accounts. For example, COSMIC Desktop (which is my default) doesn’t have an online account feature that you configure in Settings, which Penguin Mail depends on. It offers an alternative method for connecting it to Gmail (via the Google Auth Platform), but you also have to configure a domain for your Google account; otherwise, it won’t work. On the other hand, GNOME and KDE both have options in Settings to add online accounts (such as Google and Office 365). Once you add an account there, it automatically appears in Penguin Mail. If you’re using either GNOME or KDE Plasma, this won’t be a problem.

In a recent development, I connected Penguin Mail to my personal email accounts, and it worked just fine. Better than that, Penguin Mail worked so well that I’m considering making it my default. It’s smooth, elegant, simple, and reliable. Yes, I keep seeing the warning that online accounts cannot be reached, but that’s not a problem with my configured account; it’s a problem with the lack of online accounts on my desktop of choice.

Sources indicate that penguin Mail features a unified inbox, Markdown support, recipient suggestions, undo send/later send, conversation view, categories, Gmail search, and a junk filter. Penguin Mail also includes AI integration and can even use locally installed instances, such as Ollama.

Penguin Mail is free to use and open-source.

Bluemail is fast, and that is a significant draw for me. While some email clients on Linux (such as Evolution) take time to open, Bluemail opens instantly. On top of its speed, Bluemail renders HTML emails better than most Linux email clients. The UI of Bluemail has some unique options, one of which is the reply window, which looks more like you’re replying to a social media post or chatting with AI. I like that design, not only because it’s quite modern, but it’s effective and simple.

BlueMail also offers integration with GemAI, BlueMail’s native AI tool. With GemAI, you can read, write, and manage emails. You can use GemAI for free, but it limits your daily generations/use. You can also get a paid account, with pricing ranging from $3/month for the Bluemail Plus account to $8/user/month for the Team account. One knock on Bluemail is that you cannot use any other AI services; you are limited to GemAI.

There are plenty of other features found in Bluemail that really impressed me, such as instant push notifications, end-to-end encryption, and a password-protected lock screen (which is great for security-minded consumers).

The more I use Bluemail, the more I realize that this client is well suited for businesses, especially if you pay for GemAI. A Bluemail Plus account gets you desktop email backup, an email verified badge, premium themes & icons, and priority support. A Bluemail Team account adds custom domain mailboxes, a domain-verified badge, and centralized billing & control.

You can install and use the Bluemail client on Linux for free.

According to recent reports, if you were to ask me which of the above email clients I consider my top pick, I’m torn between Penguin Mail and Bluemail, but any app listed here will serve you well. I’ve recently adopted Penguin Mail as my default because it has everything I need and lets me connect to my local Ollama AI instance.
