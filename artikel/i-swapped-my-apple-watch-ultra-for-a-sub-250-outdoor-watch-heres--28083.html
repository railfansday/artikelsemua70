Industry observers note that back in October 2017, I took off my Casio G-Shock for the last time, strapped on an Apple Watch Series 3, and haven’t looked back. I worked my way up through Apple’s lineup until the Ultra was released in 2022. Today, I’m wearing an Ultra 4.

Also: I tested the Apple Watch Ultra 4, and these 4 upgrades matter most.

Given how attached I’ve become to the Apple Watch, it’s rare for me to even review any alternatives (I gave the Garmin Fenix 8 Pro a go, but it wasn’t for me), so I was surprised to find how much I liked the sub-$300 Mibro GS Explorer S Titanium, a watch from a lesser-known brand.

Industry observers note that I got to test the titanium version of the GS Explorer S, but note there’s a cheaper ceramic model that’s virtually identical except for three things — the material (titanium alloy vs. ceramic and fiber-reinforced polymer), mapping (only the titanium version has offline mapping), and of course, price.

According to recent reports, the weight is also slightly different at 0.18 oz, but that’s small enough to ignore. To keep things simple, I’m not including the titanium version with the metal band in this comparison.

Another feature that’s key to the success of a smartwatch is battery life.

Sources indicate that A few things stand out to me with this watch, both positive and negative. As an Apple Watch wearer, getting a couple of days from my Ultra is good, so I find going well over a week between charges quite remarkable. It eliminates that nightly charge, which I’d appreciate on those multi-day hikes where I’d normally take my Apple Watch charger.

Recent findings suggest that however, I’m so used to charging my Apple Watch nightly that it’s become a ritual, and switching to a weekly (or even less frequent) charging schedule makes it more likely I’ll forget. Also, it comes with a proprietary magnetic charging cable rather than a standard USB-C, which is a little more limiting. I suppose the good news is that if I lose it, there are replacements available.

I like that it comes with two bands, which gives more options. I’m a fan of orange, so that’s my choice there. And, in contrast to a lot of smartwatches, changing the band is relatively easy because it uses a custom quick-release spring bar that can be opened with a finger. Nice touch.

The Corning Gorilla Glass 3 gets a thumbs-up from me, despite being over a decade old. There are much worse materials to make a smartwatch crystal from, and I expect it to resist scratches, scrapes, and bumps well. For a rugged watch, the screen is the part most likely to get scratched, and decent protection is vital if the watch is to survive for any length of time.

In a recent development, however, it’s not invulnerable by any stretch of the imagination. And I know from experience that it won’t be anywhere near as durable as the sapphire that’s on my Apple Watch Ultra. But that’s in a totally different price category.

According to recent reports, another thumbs-up from me is the display’s 1,500-nit brightness, making it ideal for outdoor conditions. If you’re thinking of going for the titanium model to get your hands on mapping, know that the maps are limited to 31 x 31-mile (50 x 50 km) tiles, which explains the odd number. Also, while you get features such as route following, deviation alerts, one-tap return, and track-back navigation, it lacks advanced routing features like route creation and rerouting.

The mapping is also somewhat sparse. But remember, I’m from the UK and we have excellent Ordnance Survey mapping of the entire country down to 1:25,000 resolution at an incredible level of detail. So for me, anything that doesn’t show me everything from big boulders on my path feels sparse.

Compare the mapping to what I can get on my Apple Watch from the mapping service in the Outdooractive app (iOS/Android).

Recent findings suggest that the Mibro Fit app is somewhat hit-and-miss, but it’s nowhere near as bad as the reviews made it out to be, so I feel it’s improved over the last couple of years. Yes, it’s a bit clunky and rough around the edges, but it did the job.

There’s no point bothering with an outdoor smartwatch if the data you’re gathering is inaccurate or incomplete. When reviewing outdoor gadgets, conscious of — and clear about — the fact that I’m one person, and the best I can do is compare one device against another. So, when I was testing the accuracy of the heart rate, SpO2, and location data, I compared them to the Apple Watch Ultra 4.

I tested the sensors while resting, walking, and hiking, and compared the data between the two devices. Two conclusions came from my testing:.

This is an obvious question that a lot of people are going to be asking, but it comes down to this: the Apple Watch is the clear winner, with one significant, glaring caveat. From the apps to advanced health features, optional 5G, and built-in payment handling, not to mention safety features such as fall and crash detection, the Apple Watch is better… if you have an iPhone.

If you have an Android, the GS Explorer S is the better smartwatch. Compare this to, say, the Apple Watch SE 3, and you get better battery life (18 hours of normal use vs. 20 days), better GPS (single-band vs. dual-band), and better water resistance (164 feet vs. 328 feet, and the GS Explorer S is OK with diving to 98 feet). And if we’re comparing the titanium model, there’s mapping (although there are also plenty of mapping apps for the Apple Watch).

It leaves the SE 3 in the dust. I could compare it to the Ultra 4 and say you get a lot of outdoor smartwatch for a lot less, but it’s hard to make a fair comparison either way, given the major price difference and how mature the Apple Watch ecosystem is.

If you’re an Android user, the Apple Watch ecosystem isn’t on your radar, so the field is more open since flagships like the Pixel Watch 5 or Samsung Galaxy Watch 9 lean more toward being smartwatches than rugged outdoor watches (the exception being the Galaxy Watch Ultra).

It has been revealed that watches are tough to review because they are so personal. I like major watches, but you might like small ones. I like a lot of information on the display, while you might like the bare minimum. We’re all different.

What I can say about the Mibro GS Explorer S is that, for the price (which at the time of writing is $140 and $230 for the ceramic or titanium models, respectively), it’s a very good outdoor smartwatch.

If this does interest you, give thought to whether you want the ceramic or titanium version. The key difference is mapping, but I’m not fully sold on it because the map tiles are somewhat small and the routing features are limited when you’re actually on a route. Knowing this might tip you toward the ceramic model, unless you like the titanium look.

If you’re a fitness enthusiast, the GS Explorer S will give you all the stats you need to keep you on track, and more, for a very good price, and a device that should give you a good few years of service.
