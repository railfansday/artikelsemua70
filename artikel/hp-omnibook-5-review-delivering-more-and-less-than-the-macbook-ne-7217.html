HP OmniBook 5 review: Delivering more (and less) than the Macbook Neo

After the MacBook Neo permanently altered perceptions of what you can expect from a budget laptop, Windows PCs have been responding in turn with their own fleet of “budget premium” laptops at competitive prices. HP’s OmniBook 5 is one such laptop, with a vibrant OLED display, colorful build, and all-day battery starting at $649.

Also: MacBook Neo production to double in 2027 – and get its most-requested upgrade.

At this price point, though, it all comes down to trade-offs. For the Neo, you lose out on keyboard backlighting, flexible configurations (it’s locked at 8GB of memory), and a lack of ports — all things the OmniBook 5 addresses.

In a recent development, HP broke up its typical lineup of gray, corporate laptops this year with more attractive colors and builds, particularly the OmniBook X Flip 14, with a unique espresso color that caught my eye. The OmniBook 5, however, takes a bubblier approach in metallic pastel pink, blue, green, and silver colorways and a thin, lightweight chassis.

It has been revealed that at 2.56 pounds, it’s lighter than the Neo and thinner as well (as evidenced by the full-screen side-by-side image comparison on HP’s site) at just 11.7mm thick when closed. It’s not a convertible, but the screen does lay completely flat, and HP’s hinge construction is solid, with almost no wobble while typing. The laptop’s physical build doesn’t feel cheap, but there is a small amount of flex and bend, particularly on the bottom plate.

It has been revealed that the 14-inch, 2K touchscreen OLED display, however, is the first thing you notice and its standout feature. For a $649 device, it offers vivid contrast and good color separation. It’s quite a glossy display, however, so you’ll probably find yourself repositioning it as I did during my testing. At 300 nits of brightness, it won’t look great outdoors, but the 2K resolution is crisp enough to run Windows at higher resolutions.

The OmniBook 5 also addresses one of the Neo’s biggest drawbacks: the lack of an HDMI port. Here, you’ve got one on the laptop’s left side, along with two USB-C (not Thunderbolt) ports and an audio jack. But sorry, you’ll have to do without a USB-A.

Recent findings suggest that the crisp display, bright colors, and responsive keyboard and trackpad all work together to make this laptop feel premium and enjoyable to use — addressing the main complaints consumers typically had of budget Windows PCs: that they’re thick and bulky with uninspiring builds and dingy displays. For $649, you’re looking at a much better-looking laptop that actually has some character. But how does it perform?

Here is where the trade-offs become a little more evident. The HP OmniBook 5 comes with an Intel Core 5 Series 3 processor — a step below its “Ultra” lineup of consumer chips, emphasizing battery life over power. The starting configuration at $649 also comes with 8GB of RAM, the bare minimum I’d suggest in 2027, with the 16GB model starting at $899.

It’s a good price, but 8GB of memory in Windows is not the same as 8GB in MacOS. This isn’t just conjecture; even Microsoft knew this was the case, as evidenced by its recent changes to speed and memory improvements in Windows 11. The OmniBook 5 I was given for testing was the 16GB model, but I have tested other devices with 8GB of memory, and there are still performance limitations.

According to recent reports, I’d suggest the 8GB model for customers who only need to handle the basics on this device: checking email, browsing the web, and working with office and productivity software. With 16GB, you’ll have a lot more options, including creative tasks like photo and video editing — even some gaming. I ran “WoW Forever” (a new game that’s not especially graphically intensive) on the OmniBook 5 with max graphics settings — it played surprisingly well, with bright colors popping on the OLED panel.

Industry observers note that when it comes to raw power, this laptop performs similarly to its competitors, emphasizing longevity and battery life, making a solid option for students, teens, and everyday users. For pros and creators looking for more horsepower to run intensive workflows, HP has plenty of other options, such as the aforementioned HP OmniBook X Flip 14 or the HP EliteBook 8 G1a on the higher end.

It has been revealed that speaking of longevity, battery life is fantastic, with the OmniBook 5 lasting just about the entire workday on one charge — and that’s after multiple calls throughout the day. The better-than-expected battery life is due to HP pairing a large 60Whr cell with the Intel Core 5 — a very power-efficient chip. HP cites up to 42 hours during its video playback test, but I’d expect an average of 8-10 hours of regular use on one charge.

Even better is the 60Wh mini GaN wall charger, which looks like it should power your smartphone — not your laptop. This tiny charger makes the OmniBook 5 even easier to commute with, adding almost no weight or bulk to the total package.

One last thing to note about this laptop is the very limited on-board storage, which solidifies its use case as a cloud-first laptop for the basics. The 8GB configuration starts with a 256GB SSD, upgradable to 512GB — which is the minimum I’d suggest in 2026, especially on a Windows PC. At 256GB, you’ll just be especially limited with the apps you can install, reducing the laptop’s longevity over time.

Compared to the MacBook Neo, the HP OmniBook 5 offers 60% more battery capacity, an HDMI port, a backlit keyboard, and a touchscreen OLED display for the same price. However, 8GB of RAM in Windows is a little more limiting than 8GB of RAM in MacOS, and there’s no way around it. This is not a dig on the OmniBook 5, it’s just the reality of Windows 11.

Recent findings suggest that performance for the tasks it’s designed for is quite good, but you need to be confident your intended use case doesn’t extend into more demanding tasks or require several hundred gigs of storage for large apps. The HP OmniBook 5 is a colorful, solid “budget premium” laptop that addresses the MacBook Neo’s trade-offs, but makes its own in the process.
