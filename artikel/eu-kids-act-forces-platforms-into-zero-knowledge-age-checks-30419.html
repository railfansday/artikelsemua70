Europe’s social media giants could soon be required to prove, not just claim, that children are kept off their services.

On September 17, 2026, the European Commission proposed the EU KIDS Act (Keeping Internet Digital Spaces Accountable and Trustworthy), a sweeping framework that would set a harmonised minimum age of 15 for independent accounts on social media and video-sharing platforms with risky features such as live streaming, contact with strangers and profiling-based feeds.

Recent findings suggest that according to Hopae, the proposal establishes three tiers. Children under 13 would be barred from holding accounts altogether, while 13 to 14 year olds could use guardian-managed accounts with parental controls permanently enabled, approved contacts and a one-hour daily cap.

Users aged 15 and over could hold their own accounts on services that must be safe by design. The Commission frames this as reversing the burden of proof, so providers must demonstrate their services are age-appropriate rather than regulators showing they are not.

In a recent development, the scope extends well beyond social networks to online games, AI companions and conversational chatbots, app stores and operating systems. Fines could reach 6% of total worldwide annual turnover, with platform penalties enforced through the Digital Services Act.

Recent findings suggest that at the heart of the proposal is age verification. New users on in-scope platforms must be checked using a certified EU age verification solution, and the Commission’s Q&A states that self-declared age is “explicitly not enough.” Approved routes include the EU age verification app, compliant European Digital Identity Wallets and certified third-party tools. The Q&A adds that “platforms do not check identity documents and do not learn who you are.”.

Crucially, Article 28(3) states that “any age assurance measure shall be zero knowledge proof,” a technology the Commission says “cannot identify, locate, track or profile anyone” using it. Platforms would receive only a yes or no answer on whether a user meets the threshold.

This raises the bar for common methods. Facial age estimation would not satisfy the account age gate, and NIST testing found its mean absolute error for children “is around 1.25–1.55 years for ages 0–17,” according to Biometric Update. ID photo uploads, meanwhile, expose full identity data and are vulnerable to forgery.

The proposal still needs approval from the European Parliament and the Council. Rules would apply six months after entry into force, with under-15 accounts disabled six months later. Greece’s digital governance minister Dimitris Papastergiou has estimated the EU process at roughly 20 months, pointing to late 2028 implementation.

In a recent development, meanwhile, national rules are fragmenting the landscape. Greece will bar under-15s from January 1, 2027, Denmark plans a limit of 15 from July 2027, and Germany is weighing an interim threshold of 13. Outside Europe, Australia already enforces an under-16 rule with fines of up to A$109.2m.

Identity verification provider Hopae says its Hopae Connect platform supports zero-knowledge age proofs built on Google’s Longfellow ZK library, the proof system named in the EU app’s specification.
