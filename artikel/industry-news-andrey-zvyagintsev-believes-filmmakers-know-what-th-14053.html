The director of Minotaur claims there is no politics in Russia.

Industry observers note that the year is 2022. Vladimir Putin has announced the military draft. In Andrey Zvyagintsev’s Minotaur, set in the fictional town of Krasoborsk, a quietly unhappy couple, called Gleb and Galina, carry on with their lives, or try to, geopolitics notwithstanding. For Galina, carrying on means an affair, which we deduce from a scene of her texting with a faint smile, her face lit in the dark by the light of her phone. (Minotaur is a remake of Claude Chabrol’s 1969 film The Unfaithful Wife, whose title gives the plot away more readily than Zvyagintsev’s.).

Recent findings suggest that meanwhile, Gleb runs a kind of private contracting concern and is soon asked to scare up military recruits to meet a quota. At this point, the domestic noir and the workplace drama merge to dramatic, devastating, and darkly funny effects.

It has been revealed that in Minotaur, as in his five other features, Zvyagintsev has the camera moving seemingly objectively, almost omnisciently, putting the characters in their context. Occasionally they appear rather small, especially when set against the epic scale of their mythic and biblical fates. That the environment shapes the person seems to be a recurring revelation across all six of his staggering films. Minotaur, too, opens with a natural landscape of the arboreal kind and ends with another, different landscape, of clouds. In the middle is a movie about people trying to make sense of the landscapes they find themselves in, of the social, moral, relational variety.

It has been revealed that zvyagintsev has some experience with finding oneself in incomprehensible landscapes. After receiving Russia’s Sputnik V covid-19 vaccine during the pandemic, he fell ill, and later learned about the invasion of Ukraine while recovering from a medical coma in Germany. He subsequently chose not to return home, moving to France.

It has been revealed that at the Cannes Film Festival in May, Minotaur won the second-highest honor. In his acceptance speech, Zvyagintsev spoke about the war, saying: “The only person who can stop this butchery is the President of the Russian Federation. Put an end to this carnage. The whole world is waiting for it.” While he resists the identity of exile, in September the Russian government designated him a “foreign agent.”.

In a recent development, zvyagintsev returned this year to the Newly introduced York Film Festival, where he last visited with his 2014 film Leviathan, which won a Golden Globe and was nominated for an Oscar. His debut in 2003, The Return, won a top prize at Venice International Film Festival, after which he was invited to meet Putin, an account of which appears in his forthcoming book. Out of his six films, four have won prestigious prizes at Cannes, including Elena and Loveless.

According to recent reports, we discussed the three years he spent cleaning the streets of Moscow in the early 1990s, his status as a Russian immigrant in France now, and how he captures that great Chekhovian instinct: that life is wretched, but what can we do, we must go on living. Zvyagintsev spoke to The Verge through a translator from Los Angeles.

Industry observers note that I understand you went from cleaning streets to making commercials. What happened in between?

It kind of happened almost at the same time, because I was cleaning the streets to be able to live in Moscow. From 1990 to 1993, I was cleaning the streets. And then I was going to the Museum of Cinema and watching a movie every single day. Those were my formative years, when I learned a lot about cinema.

I had been living in a huge communal flat, free accommodation in a beautiful historic mansion in central Moscow. It was a really massive apartment, and there were three street cleaners, including me, that lived there. In 1993, I was forced to leave the apartment after someone from the local housing administration decided to take it over. The two others had families, so they decided that if someone was supposed to move, it was going to be me, since I was single.

I was offered alternative accommodation, but realized that the only reason I had continued working as a street sweeper was to keep that particular apartment.

So the first night when I had to leave the apartment, I left it fast without an actual plan. I had to spend the night inside a truck that was abandoned and that was parked in the street.

I got a latest job as a guard, as a night guard at a kindergarten where I also ate leftover food from the children’s meals.

After the first day, I called my friend who was working in commercials and doing music videos. I asked him to help with a job, because I was sure I was able to shoot commercials. After the first commercial, I met some producers who liked my work, and then I started shooting commercials on the regular and working as a freelance for some time. One of those commercials was noticed by a producer from a TV channel called REN TV, who was shooting a bunch of TV series at the time. His name was Dmitry Lesnevsky and he asked me whether I wanted to shoot movies, and I said it was my dream. And then Dmitry presented me with 15 scripts of choice, and I shot three episodes of a television series called The Black Room for this channel, each 25 minutes long.

Sources indicate that we were army friends. He was a close friend from the army. He was a cinema mechanic. He was starting movies for the soldiers when they were in the army. They had a theater, and I was in the music ensemble in the army. He was also an actor.

You had an adverse reaction to a covid vaccine. You were ill for a long time. You nearly died. You moved, and eventually settled in France. You’ve stated you can’t know how that experience changed you as a filmmaker, but I’m curious if you know how that experience changed you as a person.

In a recent development, I got sick in Russia. I was in a coma for 40 days in Germany. I had a major problem with my lungs, a bacteria called klebsiella found its way there. They thought that they would need a lung transplant. Fortunately, the Germans were able to operate, and the transplant was not needed. I moved to France a year later after the coma.

Sources indicate that what was the biggest change for me was the rhythm of life. I understood that you cannot lose time, so I’m trying to do things in a much more dynamic manner, and not leave things for later because we don’t know if this later exists. So that that’s the biggest change, really, just seeing life as something that passes by promptly.

Recent findings suggest that you are a vocal opponent of the current Russian government. Do you consider yourself to be in political exile?

Industry observers note that I am not in political exile. I did not have that status, and I never applied for it because I don’t see any necessity for that. I consider myself an immigrant. I’ve been living away from my native country for almost five years, but I did not leave on purpose. I was already in Europe when the war happened. There is a term in Russian, called nevozvrashchenets, which means a person who does not come back, someone who has left their home country and has not returned, particularly for political reasons. I was in the clinic in Germany and then in France when the war started, and I realized that I could not go back to the country that is at war.

You’ve remarked you are not political, but your movies seem overtly political, to me. You’ve also said that the job of an artist is to reflect reality. You don’t think you’re political, but your job is to reflect reality, and we are living at a time when politics is dominant. How do you reconcile all this?

Industry observers note that it’s very simple to reconcile. It’s just that the politics barges into our lives, and we have to reflect what’s going on in the world. You become a witness of what’s going on beyond your window, what’s going on to the environment, to the world around you, to the world close to you. It just so happens that the politics dominate our lives. We are witnessing historical events that we cannot not reflect in the movies.

“There is no politics in Russia, in the sense of politics being a struggle for power, dialogue between different parties or worldviews… This does not exist in Russia. There’s only one power”.

In a recent development, when I say I’m not a political animal, I mean that I have no way of influencing the political process in my country, especially while the war is going on. There is no politics in Russia, in the sense of politics being a struggle for power, dialogue between different parties or worldviews, you know, left, right, Democrats, Republicans. This does not exist in Russia. There’s only one power.

It feels more like domination in the way Foucault meant it.

Yes. It’s not even that there is only one party that dominates. It’s that no one even questions it, and no one even tries to struggle. It’s kind of a totalitarian regime at this time.

Industry observers note that let’s talk Minotaur. I believe you said you wanted to adapt Ingrid Bergman first, but when that didn’t go, you asked your producers for other available properties and they sent you a list. You also said you asked for marital dramas from the ’60s and ’70s specifically. Why?

I wanted to do a remake of a movie that would have three or four main characters, not a lot of different locations, that would be a little more compact, because there was a certain budget consideration. The films that I had previously considered, the scripts I considered, were more expensive, and so I was not able to get any of them going.

And that’s when I asked for the list from my friend who is French, and he gave me a list of French films. If I had an Italian friend, those would have been Italian movies.

I asked him for the best films from the ’60s and ’70s because it’s far away enough for us, those were probably the films that were a little bit more forgotten by the public, or a new generation of the public maybe hasn’t seen them. And that’s how I discovered Claude Chabrol’s The Unfaithful Wife.

You also stated that something about the Chabrol did not work for you, and that’s what gave you the inspiration to want to remake it.

In a recent development, I wouldn’t formulate it as something not working, but I would rather say that he made a kind of a formal choice when it comes to the office life of the main character. Chabrol concentrates more on the crime plot and the love triangle [than the office life], which is totally his right.

And I thought it was something that I would like to fill in. In 2022, the war was going on, and then in September they started the partial mobilization in Russia, and so this plot kind of just barged into our movie, and filled out that space that was empty previously.

Your movies are full of scenes Hollywood executives would ask to cut. I am thinking of the ending of Minotaur, with the clouds, or The Banishment, which ends with women collecting hay and they are singing. What is the effect of these moments in your movies?

It would be impossible to cut out those moments.

What are they doing in the movie?

When you say that Hollywood execs would probably like to cut that stuff out, the viewers, including viewers in Hollywood, really like the clouds [that Minotaur ends on], for example, and they even tell me that the sequence could have been longer. So I want to tell you that the execs do not know what the viewers want because when the viewer goes to the cinema, he kind of goes to sleep and then he watches a dream. And as long as this dream is aesthetically pleasing and harmonious and beautiful, that’s what the viewers should be watching, and no one should cut that out.

It has been revealed that this is the key conflict between the producer and the creator. The producer does not trust the creator to know what the audience wants, but the creator knows. And so, if the producer is so sure of himself, he should write a script himself.

Sources indicate that you published a book about the making of your third film, Elena. I understand you’re working on a second book. What can you tell us about this project? Will it include advice to young filmmakers?

The whole book, in a way, will be an advice to younger filmmakers, or people who love movies, because it will be a very detailed account of the six films that I have released so far, six conversations with one of the best cinema critics writing in Russian, Anton Dolin.

Would you ever work in another language apart from Russian or with Netflix?

According to recent reports, yes, I am open. In fact, I have a script in English. Two even.
