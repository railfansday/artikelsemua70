Introducing MedChemBench, a benchmark that tests how well AI models read molecular structures, interpret experimental results, and choose which compounds to test next.

According to recent reports, we’re introducing MedChemBench, a benchmark that evaluates AI models on reasoning tasks drawn from medicinal chemistry research. It tests whether models can connect a molecule’s structure with experimental evidence to explain results, compare compounds, and predict their properties. Building AI that can reliably support this work is one step towards helping scientists develop better medicines.

One of the hardest parts of this work is that improving one property of a molecule can make another worse. A compound might act strongly against a disease related protein but be poorly absorbed or break down too swiftly in the body. Medicinal chemists work through these tradeoffs by designing, making, and testing related compounds, then deciding what to try next. For AI to help, it needs to connect changes in molecular structure with experimental evidence and reason about what compounds are worth pursuing.

It has been revealed that medChemBench measures these abilities through 99 problems drawn from published research. At the xhigh reasoning setting, GPT‑6.2 Astra earned 84.5% of the available credit, compared with 83.3% for GPT‑6 Astra, 82.0% for GPT‑6.1 Sol, and 58.2% for GPT‑5.6 Sol. GPT‑6 Astra was strongest at reconstructing molecular structures and explaining experimental evidence. Selecting compounds and predicting their numerical properties proved harder for that model.

It has been revealed that medChemBench draws on 117 papers and patents and includes 761 distinct molecular structures. For most problems, models receive the context available at a chosen point in a research program: the biological target, the researchers’ goals, the compounds studied so far, and selected experimental findings.

The benchmark evaluates four components:.

Industry observers note that molecular structures. Correct atom connections and specified stereochemistry in molecules assembled from the drawings and chemical groups provided.

Scientific reasoning. Explanations supported by the supplied evidence, such as the reasons for choosing one compound over another.

Ranking and selection. Compound rankings and choices that meet the stated requirements.

In a recent development, property predictions. Agreement with the numerical values and categories reported in the source material, including measured and calculated molecular properties.

Recent findings suggest that one MedChemBench problem draws on research into inhibitors of PRMT5, a protein studied in cancer drug discovery. The model receives molecular structures and results from earlier experiments. It must predict how three related compounds will behave: how strongly they block PRMT5 in cells, how readily they cross a cell barrier, and how promptly liver enzymes break them down in a laboratory assay.Later experiments, withheld from the model, demonstrated a tradeoff. Compound 23 blocked PRMT5 in cells at a lower concentration, while compound 24 crossed the cell barrier more readily and broke down more slowly in the liver-enzyme assay.

In a recent development, predicting these properties together tests whether a model can connect molecular structures with several aspects of compound behavior. This reflects a central challenge in medicinal chemistry: stronger activity against a target does not necessarily come with better permeability or slower breakdown.The benchmark checks whether the model reconstructs the molecular structures correctly and how closely its predictions match experimental measurements. Predictions receive partial credit based on their closeness to the measured values. Measurements already supplied in the prompt must also be reproduced correctly.

Industry observers note that small chemical changes can produce different property profiles. The task asks models to connect the structures with evidence about their behavior.

Before a model can reason about a compound’s properties, it needs to identify the molecule correctly. Chemical drawings encode atom connections, ring structures, and stereochemistry.

MedChemBench tests whether models preserve this information when reconstructing molecules from the drawings and chemical groups provided.

One problem asks models to assemble a molecule from a central structure and two attached groups while preserving an 18-atom ring. At the highest reasoning setting shared by all eleven models (xhigh), GPT‑6 Astra reconstructed this compound correctly in all ten responses. GPT‑5.6 Sol and GPT‑5.6 Luna failed across all ten responses.

In the illustrated answers, GPT‑5.6 Sol produced a 15-atom ring, while GPT‑5.6 Luna split the molecule into two disconnected fragments.

According to recent reports, these errors change the identity of the molecule being analyzed. This example tests a foundation of medicinal chemistry reasoning: whether the model can accurately represent the compound before interpreting its behavior.

According to recent reports, while measuring the benchmark for structure components, Astra earned 95.4% of the available credit at xhigh. This result shows strong performance on reconstructing the molecular structures supplied in the tasks.

We evaluated eleven GPT models at different reasoning-effort settings. Across the full benchmark, Astra earned 83.3% of the available credit at the shared xhigh setting, compared with 82.0% for GPT‑6.1 Sol, 75.1% for GPT‑6.1 Terra, and 58.2% for GPT‑5.6 Sol. It has been revealed that astra’s clearest strengths were in reconstructing molecular structures and explaining the scientific evidence. In selected reasoning examples from our preprint, Astra correctly identified structural changes and weighed their possible effects on protein binding, absorption, and metabolism.

Where selection and prediction remain difficult, Astra earned 95.4% of the available structure credit at xhigh, but only about half the credit for selecting compounds and predicting numerical properties. These categories use different tasks and scoring rules, but the results show substantial room to improve predictions and compound choices.

According to recent reports, these results do not establish whether models can discover new drugs or improve laboratory decisions. Prior exposure to the published sources cannot be ruled out, and each response was a single attempt without tools, feedback, or new experiments. While the benchmark evaluates reasoning from historical evidence rather than real-world drug discovery, its results help identify where models are advancing and where challenges remain. We believe MedChemBench will support continued progress and look forward to sharing updates as our models advance to serve the field.
