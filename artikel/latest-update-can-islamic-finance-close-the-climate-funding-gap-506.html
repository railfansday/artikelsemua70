Latest Update: Can Islamic finance close the climate funding gap?

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

As COP31 gets under way in Antalya, the climate agenda has moved from pledges to delivery. Islamic finance holds an estimated $6.5tn in assets and is expected to reach $10.5tn by 2030, so it is increasingly hard to ignore as a source of climate capital. However, new analysis from LSEG Data & Analytics suggests the sector’s biggest hurdle may be expertise rather than money.

According to recent reports, türkiye is the fourth member of the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation (OIC) to host the UN climate summit in five years. That timing matters. According to LSEG Data & Analytics, much of Islamic finance’s expected growth is concentrated in markets with heavy climate investment needs. These range from hydrocarbon producers trying to diversify their economies to countries already highly exposed to climate impacts.

LSEG Data & Analytics argues that the two fields are a natural match. Islamic finance is guided by Maqasid al-Shariah, the higher objectives of Islamic law, which focus on protecting human welfare. It also treats people as stewards of the earth, which places environmental protection firmly within its ethical framework. Structurally, Islamic financing must be linked to real assets or economic activity. This makes it well suited to tangible projects such as renewable energy, water infrastructure and green buildings.

Sources indicate that sukuk, the Islamic equivalent of bonds, form a market worth more than $1tn. They appeal to investors who can only hold Shariah-compliant assets, and they are also familiar to conventional investors. As a result, a single issuance can tap both pools of capital.

ESG sukuk have grown steadily since the first deal in 2017, reaching $76.7bn in total by Q3 2026, LSEG Data & Analytics found. Issuance hit a record $24.9bn in 2025, a 54% increase on 2024.

According to recent reports, momentum has cooled this year. Issuance reached only $11.4bn by the end of Q3 2026, as many corporates delayed deals because of ongoing geopolitical tensions. Corporates accounted for 59% of ESG sukuk issuance in 2025, so their hesitation has had an outsized effect on the market.

In a recent development, LSEG Data & Analytics nevertheless sees this as a pause rather than a reversal. It points to three reasons for optimism. The first is a growing pipeline, as net-zero targets and diversification plans across the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) will require sustained investment over the next five to 15 years.

The second is that frameworks are already in place, with sovereigns in the GCC, Malaysia, Indonesia and Türkiye having published green or sustainability financing frameworks that cover sukuk. Indonesia already issues sovereign green sukuk regularly.

Sources indicate that the third is a widening scope, with Malaysia issuing the world’s first two blue sukuk this year to fund water infrastructure. This pushes Islamic climate finance into adaptation, a pressing issue in markets such as Indonesia and Pakistan.

In a recent development, even so, LSEG Data & Analytics is realistic about the limits. ESG sukuk remain a small share of global ESG bond issuance, and Islamic finance alone will not close the global climate finance gap.

Recent findings suggest that the case is far stronger in markets where Islamic finance is already established, but obstacles remain. Project bankability is one of them. Another is the challenge issuers, arrangers and investors face in handling eligibility criteria, external reviews, and allocation and impact reporting on top of Shariah compliance. As COP31 focuses on implementation, closing that capability gap could prove as crucial as the capital itself.
