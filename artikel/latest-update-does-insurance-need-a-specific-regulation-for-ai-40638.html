Latest Update: Does insurance need a specific regulation for AI?

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

As artificial intelligence adoption continues to grow across insurance, should there be specific regulations for its use within the sector?

Traditionally, regulators have been slow to respond to new innovations as they enter the market. However, AI is a different kettle of fish. Given the pace of change around AI and its growing impact on the way businesses operate, regulators have been taking a more active approach to overseeing the technology. The most notable is the EU AI Act, which is the world’s first regulation entirely focused on AI and establishes a risk-based AI classification system. Other regulators are either looking at building their own frameworks or strengthening existing regulations to encompass AI.

Sources indicate that it is no longer a question of whether insurance will embrace AI; it already has. A recent report from Goldman Sachs, which surveyed 434 Chief Investment Officers and Chief Financial Officers, found that 62% of firms are already using AI within their operations. This is up from 48% in 2025 and 29% in 2024.

Sources indicate that not only is the technology already a part of the insurance world, but it has use cases across the entire value chain. An EIOPA report looking specifically at generative AI claimed that 40% of firms are using the technology in their customer service, with an additional 48% planning to leverage the technology here. Claims management was the second most popular space for generative AI, with 32% of firms already using the technology for these processes and another 59% planning to use it. The technology also had adoption across pricing and underwriting, sales and distribution, product development, and fraud detection.

Sources indicate that given the penetration of AI within wealth management, it could be inevitable that insurance-specific regulations will start to take shape. Matt Twist, VP Sales EMEA at Earnix, believes that more clarification is needed, but not necessarily upcoming regulation.

He stated that existing regulations can provide an key foundation for the use of AI, but greater clarification and guidance is needed.

“Existing regulation provides an important foundation, but as Artificial Intelligence (AI) becomes increasingly embedded into insurance pricing, rating and underwriting, clearer guidance is needed on how existing obligations around consumer protection, fairness and accountability should apply.”.

AI can be a powerful tool for analysing complex and vast datasets, improving risk management and accelerating response times. However, insurers will still need to explain and justify any outputs from an AI tool. Blackbox solutions are not going to be acceptable.

Twist noted, “This is particularly crucial in the UK, where the FCA’s Consumer Duty and existing insurance pricing requirements place emphasis on customer outcomes and fair treatment. Insurers need confidence that the data informing their decisions is appropriate, that models perform as intended and that pricing outcomes can be evidenced and reviewed.”.

As such, Twist doesn’t think there needs to be an entirely latest regulation that is just focused on AI. Instead, clear, consistent guidance that addresses specific risks associated with AI, will be just as beneficial to the sector. These would include bias, explainability, data quality and ongoing model performance.

Melanie Hayes, co-founder at KYND, was less certain on whether the sector needed specific regulations or whether smaller guidance would be sufficient. However, much like Twist, she was clear that AI’s use will depend on the certainty and auditability of answers that it produces.

She stated that within underwriting, fairness, accountability and explainability are just as important whether it is a human or AI involved in the process.

Industry observers note that hayes said, “We’re likely to see more strain in how it’s applied in practice. For example, as AI moves into pricing and underwriting, it gets harder to show how a decision was reached – particularly when the tool belongs to a third party. Whether that’s best answered by latest rules or by clearer guidance on existing ones, the underlying need is the same: insurers have to be able to evidence their decisions.

Industry observers note that “From where we sit, that starts with the data. Insurance runs on decisions that have to be defended later, to a regulator, a reinsurer or a policyholder, and an AI output is only as defensible as the information behind it.”.

What would be in a specific regulation for AI use within insurance?

If a regulator decided to tackle AI’s use within insurance through a specific regulation, what should be included? The technology is highly adaptability and use cases across the majority of operations. If the regulation is too lax, it could become ineffective, while overly prescriptive rules could stifle innovation and adoption.

For Hayes, there are three areas that could be worth exploring. The first concerns the inputs, including where they came from, when they were observed and whether they can be verified. “A model can be entirely transparent about its logic and still get the risk wrong if it’s working from data that is out of date, incorrect, or inferred but treated as fact.”.

The second area Hayes highlighted was explainability at the level of an individual decision. If a broker or policyholder asks why a risk was declined or loaded, the ability to point to something specific or observable behind it becomes vital. “In cyber, that kind of evidence is increasingly available, so it seems a reasonable expectation to explore more widely.”.

Finally, Hayes remarked regulators could explore how the tool works with suppliers. A majority of AI used within insurance is an external third-party solution, rather than an internally built AI. Hayes said, “it’s worth thinking about how the same expectations on data and explainability would apply to the tools they buy, and what evidence an insurer could reasonably ask a supplier for.”.

Twist had his own four areas that he sees making up the core of an insurance AI regulation: transparency, fairness, accountability and continuous monitoring.

Firstly, insurers should have the ability to explain how AI informs pricing and underwriting decisions. Earnix Price-It, for example, helps pricing teams develop and evaluate strategies via modelling, optimisation and simulation. A regulation should encourage insurers to understand what factors influence decisions, assess potential outcomes prior to deployment and maintain the evidence needed for auditing.

Sources indicate that secondly, the regulation should establish the expectations around data governance and fairness. He stated, “AI models are only as reliable as the data and assumptions underpinning them. Insurers need robust processes to validate data quality, identify potential bias and monitor performance across different customer groups. This is particularly key when machine learning is used to identify patterns that traditional approaches may not capture.”.

According to recent reports, in terms of accountability, Twist noted that as insurers adopt generative and agentic AI into their operational workflows, regulation should clarify the expectations around permissions, human intervention, traceability and responsibility for outcomes. “The use of AI must not create ambiguity about who is accountable for a decision.”.

Finally, regulation would need to encourage ongoing monitoring, rather than relying solely on approval at the point of deployment. Twist stated, “Risk profiles, customer behaviour and market conditions change, meaning models must be reviewed throughout their lifecycle. Solutions such as Earnix’s Enterprise Rating Engine support rapid implementation of pricing changes, making it key that the governance surrounding those decisions keeps pace with the speed of deployment.”.

Industry observers note that ultimately, the requirements will need to be proportionate to the risk and use of the technology. Not all AI use cases carry the same level of risk and regulation should not enforce a blanket level of rules on all uses.

According to recent reports, twist said, “AI supporting an internal administrative task should not automatically face the same level of scrutiny as a system directly influencing a customer’s premium or access to insurance. A risk-based approach would help protect customers without creating unnecessary barriers to adoption.”.

An accelerator or a barrier to innovation.

When it comes to regulation some consider it a barrier to innovation and others that consider it a necessary support system.

Sources indicate that for Hayes, it all depends on how the regulation is designed. She noted that uncertainty is one of the biggest brakes on using AI within insurance and while a tool might work well in a pilot, production can still stall if uncertainty surrounds what regulators might demand when a decision is challenged.

“When the rules are unclear, caution is a rational response, so clearer expectations could give firms more confidence to invest.”.

Sources indicate that if the rules are too broad, this could slow down innovation, Hayes explained. Treating all models as the same will add cost to low-risk users without making high-risk ones any safer. The hardest hit would be the smaller firms with fewer compliance resources.

She added, “It’s also worth thinking about the effect beyond the insurer itself. If regulation gives businesses more confidence to adopt AI at scale, the same handful of models and providers could end up sitting behind a growing share of every book. That kind of concentration is something insurers may want visibility of across their portfolios.”.

Twist held a similar opinion that a well-designed regulation will accelerate AI adoption if it gives firms clarity and confidence to move beyond experiments.

He said, “Our 2026 Trends Report, The Race to Reinvent, highlighted that there is a growing momentum behind AI, alongside the challenges insurers face in adopting it responsibly.

Recent findings suggest that “For example, 81% of respondents reported that AI was integrated into workflows across some or most functions, while 83% expressed concern that their AI models were trained on incomplete or inaccurate data. This demonstrates that adoption is progressing, but the foundations needed to support reliable, responsible AI are not always keeping pace.”.

Recent findings suggest that regulation will help to narrow this gap. Clear expectations for data quality, transparency, privacy and model governance will mean insurers understand what is needed to deploy AI confidently.

In a recent development, however, Twist believes regulation will need to be proportionate and adaptable. Citing Earnix’s research, 56% of respondents favour a gradual approach to AI adoption, keeping human intervention as a key pillar over the coming years. Human accountability and safeguards will need to be established, but overly prescriptive rules could impact experimentation or promptly become outdated as the technology evolves.

Sources indicate that he concluded, “Ultimately, regulation should be an enabler, not a brake on progress. Our report revealed that insurers which align AI adoption with stronger data practices, regulatory readiness and customer-focused strategies will be better positioned to turn experimentation into measurable business value.

“By combining clear regulatory expectations with modern technology and embedded governance, the industry can accelerate innovation, strengthen customer trust and make faster, more informed decisions.”.
