For Dr. Kate Goodrich, who oversees clinician enablement through technology at Optum, the problem of disconnected healthcare is a personal one. She spent about 14 years helping manage care for her mother, a Medicare patient with multiple chronic conditions who took around 17 medications. Despite Dr. Goodrich’s background as a physician and healthcare policymaker, coordinating between specialists, pharmacies, and labs was still difficult.

It has been revealed that her lived experience is just one example of a much bigger challenge facing healthcare. Patients, clinicians, and health plans often work across disconnected systems, creating extra steps and delays along the way. By leveraging AI thoughtfully, Optum’s approach isn’t to add another layer to an already complicated system, but instead make data and technology work together more efficiently and harmoniously.

Here are some ways Optum is improving health intelligence to help deliver quality care.

In a recent development, to help clinicians stay present, Optum leverages ambient scribe technology, which records a conversation between a clinician and patient, then uses AI to generate a clinical note in the electronic health record. This means a doctor doesn’t have to spend the entire appointment typing into a computer.

Dr. Goodrich indicates it’s one of the technology’s most vital benefits. She explains that clinicians can look at their patients, listen to what they’re saying, and stay engaged in the conversation while the documentation happens in the background. It’s a way to give doctors back some of the human connection that was lost when electronic health records became a fixture of the exam room.

In a recent development, “AI has actually allowed us to be more human,” Dr. Goodrich claims.

Documentation isn’t the only place AI can help. Preparing for an appointment can mean searching through lab results, imaging, specialist notes, hospital records, and other information scattered across multiple systems. Clinical summaries can pull that information together and give a doctor a concise overview before the appointment. Other tools can surface relevant medical evidence or suggest a next step within the clinician’s existing workflow. Healthcare providers using Optum technology to coordinate and automate these tasks saw a 10.8% increase in real-time eligibility responses and saved 298 hours of manual work along the way, according to the company.

It has been revealed that that doesn’t mean handing clinical decisions over to AI. The clinician makes the final call, taking into consideration a patient’s circumstances, preferences, and support system.

Julie Durham, Chief Information Officer of Optum Insight, describes the goal as getting “the right data, at the right moment, in the right experience.”.

Patients feel the effects of a fragmented healthcare system, too, and perhaps you’ve experienced it yourself. A referral to a specialist, a latest prescription, or a trip to the lab can mean another appointment to arrange, another system to navigate, and yet another opportunity for information to fall through the cracks.

Sources indicate that optum’s solutions are designed to connect these pieces seamlessly, bringing patient, provider, and claims information together in real time so that relevant information is available before care is delivered. It can also identify potential gaps in care and help address administrative tasks that would otherwise require phone calls, paperwork, or follow-up. The goal is to have fewer people experience what Dr. Goodrich and many others have previously experienced when trying to coordinate care, either for themselves or their loved ones.

Recent findings suggest that the future of healthcare isn’t about replacing people with technology. Optum is building technology to support patients, clinicians and care givers, reduce complexity, and create more opportunities for care that feels more personalized. When information flows more seamlessly, clinicians can focus more on patients, and patients can focus more on their health.
