I tested an open-source AI notetaker that doesn’t monitor my conversations – now I’m sold

Right now, there’s probably something you’re forgetting to do. Do you know what it is? Tech companies are wise to our absentmindedness, selling the solution back to us in the form of miced wearables that yearn to be our personal assistants.

Also: I tried the Flowtica Verso AI notetaker expecting a gimmick – now it’s my fresh favorite MagSafe gadget.

According to recent reports, over the past few weeks, I tested two such devices that claim to refresh my failing memory, but with very different approaches. One was Siri Recap, a beta feature on the latest Apple Watch that listens to your conversations and generates summaries. The other was the Pebble Index 01, a ring with a microphone and a button — and nothing else.

The two wearables illustrate how different companies are building the same product. The $75 Pebble Index 01 is open-source and easily integrated with plug-ins. Siri Recap is private and closed. The note-taking ring has one purpose. The Apple Watch has many. Consumers control what the Index 01 notes. Apple Watch users are at the whims of Siri. But which actually does the job better? I tested the Index 01 to see for myself.

Pebble’s Index 01 is not exactly a smart ring like the Oura Ring — it can’t track your heart rate or tell you how well you slept. Instead, it’s essentially a specialized mic with its own app, or a personalized agent, if that agent were wrapped around your finger. The ring comes in three colors: silver, gold, or black, and fits sizes six through 13. You can use it with or without an internet connection, and the Index 01 stores up to 5 minutes of recordings without your phone present.

Also: I watched a movie on Meta’s latest VR glasses – it was awesome, but isolating.

In a recent development, wearing the device felt like I was wearing another smart ring. While I didn’t sleep with it on, I definitely could have — and wouldn’t have noticed its presence. It was discreet and comfortable. It’s water-resistant, so I wore the ring while washing my hands and doing the dishes with no problems. It’s not rechargeable, with a battery life of around two to three years, depending on usage. Once it dies, you’ll have no choice but to buy another. Thankfully, $75 per ring isn’t a bad price.

Sources indicate that the Pebble Index 01 is a straightforward wearable. Have a stroke of genius or a grocery item you don’t want to forget? Press the button on the ring’s crown, and speak into the microphone. Your note will appear in the accompanying Pebble app, where it’s organized into shopping lists, to-dos, or customized lists.

I got a notification each time a note was recorded on the app, so I could quality-check my notes before they’re sorted, or redo any errors. The microphone didn’t pick up what was mentioned or misheard my note a few times, but these are easily editable within the app. Most of the time, the Index 01 works perfectly fine.

Recent findings suggest that the ring is designed to connect to various plugins, such as Claude Code, Muse, or Notion, so ring wearers can control where their queries go and use the device as a wearable agent. Because it’s so easy to connect to other apps, you can use the ring as if it’s an extension of your phone, asking the ring to turn on the smart lights through one app or vibe code a website in another. I connected my Index 01 to Claude, and sent it questions while my phone was out of reach.

In a recent development, I’ve found the ring to be most useful for the random stuff in my life. As I juggle work tasks, friends’ birthdays, holiday prepares, and my own growing list of to-dos, the Index 01 helped me to organize those fleeting moments where an important memory popped up I was too distracted to remember.

According to recent reports, the ring has helped me note important dates. It reminded me to ask my boyfriend about a DJ event I wanted to go to (I had even forgotten that I made that note in the first place). I’ve talked through my Saturday to-do list with the ring, organizing the list purely by voice. In fact, lists have become one of the main ways I’ve used the ring. The last list I added to consists of Christmas decorations and holiday outing ideas.

It has been revealed that one gripe is that you have to manually create fresh lists on the app. You can’t just make a new one verbally. I organize my to-do lists by date and keep separate lists for products I’ve been meaning to buy, as well as for notes to self and writing goals. These ideas are often stray thoughts I have one moment and forget the next, but the ring can capture and contain a single idea, holding me more accountable for my own creativity.

Don’t get me wrong: I’m a pen-and-paper writer through and through, and won’t be ditching my physical agenda when it comes to organizing and time-stamping my to-do list. Nevertheless, the Index 01 assisted me when I didn’t have my notepad handy — or when I thought of something I’d need to remember in the distant future.

The bottom line? The ring has certainly helped me to remember more. Pebble accomplishes this, in part, by designing the technology to quickly organize and sort spoken notes. It also helps me to remember by simply forcing me to speak notes out loud — not just think them.

According to recent reports, after testing the Index 01, I’m less convinced that ambient-listening devices are the solution to the forgetfulness crisis. The Pebble Index 01 helped me remember better because I could speak to it directly, forcing me to take an active role in choosing what to note and prioritize. With the always-listening Siri Recap, I had far less control over what Siri deemed noteworthy enough to summarize.

Sources indicate that testing Siri Recap resulted in a lot of useless conversation summaries, listening for any voices at all without discernment. At one point, it even picked up on my TikTok scrolling and made a note on it. Another time, it summarized “Clubkid,” a movie I had watched with my friend, creating a note about drug addiction and child custody, as if I were the one experiencing these things and not the main character of the movie.

In a recent development, it was always listening. In doing so, it summarized information in the app that I had no use for, or made me sift through small talk to find a conversation tidbit I was looking for.

Also: I tested Siri Recap on my Apple Watch for a week – then turned it off, for good.

Industry observers note that tech companies are pushing all-seeing and all-listening devices at consumers left and right, but my time with the Index 01 proved to me that the best technology is rarely invasive. It can be as simple as a ring with a microphone and a helpful, accompanying app.

I’d recommend the Pebble Index 01 to anyone interested in a upcoming approach to note-taking or memory jogging. This device is not only straightforward to use and easy to customize with plug-ins, but it’s also cheaper than its competitors, at $75. It helped me keep track of stray thoughts, dates, and my long list of personal and work-related to-dos.

I can genuinely say it helped me remember things I would’ve forgotten, and I appreciate its minimal approach more than Apple’s always-on listening, which both comes with its own privacy concerns and is less efficient.
