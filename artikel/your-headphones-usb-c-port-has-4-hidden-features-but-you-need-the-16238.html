Your headphones’ USB-C port has 4 hidden features – but you need the right cable

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Grab your favorite over-ear headphones and check their charging port; it’s probably USB-C. Though modern headphones use a universal USB-C charging port, the port isn’t solely for charging. Your headphones’ USB-C port can also be the gateway for lossless audio playback, low-latency voice transmission during calls, low audio/visual latency for gaming, and a vessel to download firmware.

Also: Your USB-C cables look alike, but work differently – how to tell them apart.

However, not all USB-C cables are created equal. Some are better for fast charging, while others are better suited for heavy data transfers. If you have a heavy-duty USB-C cable on hand, it’ll work great for charging and audio transmission, but its stiff, heavy build can rub against your earcups, causing unwanted noise.

Here’s what you should know before reaching for any old USB-C port and plugging it into your headphones.

Premium headphones and upper-midrange headphones usually offer lossless-to-hi-res audio via a wired USB-C connection. Except Sony, which still has full loyalty to the 3.5mm jack. So, if you want higher-quality audio than what compressed Bluetooth audio can offer, you can plug a USB-C cable into your headphones and start listening.

Industry observers note that you can often unlock up to 24-bit/96kHz with USB-C audio-enabled headphones, and some models support streaming up to 24-bit/192kHz. Since the headphones’ USB-C port serves as a data, audio, and charging interface, you can often listen at high resolution while charging them.

In a recent development, if you’re a serious mobile gamer, Bluetooth’s audio compression technology introduces lag in audio-visual responsiveness. Headphones streaming audio over the SBC or AAC codecs experience between 120 and 250 ms of latency, which doesn’t sound like a lot but is noticeable in fast-paced gaming. You can reduce latency to 30-50 ms with a wired USB-C connection.

It has been revealed that for voice calling and video conferencing, clearer voice pickup can make your meetings smoother and ensure you’re heard clearly by others. USB-C audio enables high-quality audio to reach your headphones, and the lack of wireless compression keeps your voice sounding clear and natural.

Voice calling or video conferencing with headphones and a PC connected wirelessly defaults to Bluetooth’s legacy Hands-Free Profile, which introduces latency, audio dropouts, and the robotic-sounding voice we all hate to hear.

If you ever want to update your headphones to the latest firmware version the old-fashioned way, you can do so via USB-C. Downloading firmware via over-the-air Bluetooth is convenient, but depending on your connection stability, it can take a while or fail midway through.

In a recent development, many headphone brands support downloading firmware via USB-C by plugging your headphones into a Mac or PC. This method is faster and more reliable, as you’re less likely to experience a stalled update.

Sources indicate that when you purchase your headphones, they come with a USB-C-to-USB-C cable, and sometimes a USB-C-to-3.5mm cable, or a 3.5mm-to-2.5mm cable. The cables that come with your headphones are the ones you should use. The included cable has the right specs for that pair of headphones’ charging and audio transmission capabilities.

It has been revealed that but if you’re like me, you have a drawer full of tangled cables and are more likely to win the lottery than identify which cable came with which device. In that case, you don’t want to use a random USB-C cable with your headphones.

Recent findings suggest that headphones draw significantly less power than smartphones or laptops, so you don’t want to use a beefed-up, supercharged USB-C cable to charge your headphones. If you’re plugging into a wall, your adapter shouldn’t exceed more than 5V.

Many USB-C cables deliver 60W to your headphones for fast charging, which is more than enough, but modern headphones also have internal power management chips that negotiate power and draw only what they need from your cable.

Check your manufacturer’s manual to see if your headphones support fast charging; if they don’t, more power won’t make them charge faster. You’ll just blow your headphones.

Most wireless consumer headphones that support USB-C audio have an internal digital-to-analog converter (DAC), which converts digital signals into sound; you don’t need to buy an external DAC.

In a recent development, with that said, audio transmission is not a power-intensive task: lossless CD-quality requires around 2 Mbps and hi-res lossless audio around 10 Mbps at most. So, you don’t need a monster of a 40 Gbps Thunderbolt to plug into your headphones. As long as your cable supports 60 W charging and USB 2.0 for data transmission, you’re all good. Industry observers note that you also want to avoid heavy, thick, powerful cables, as their weight can pull on and bump your earcups, causing an annoying thudding sound. Look for soft, thinner, flexible cables. The cables that came with your headphones check all the right boxes for that specific manufacturer, so my advice is to keep them in their carrying case to avoid them getting mixed up with others.
