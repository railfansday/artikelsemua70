Tech Report: Why carbon data alone won’t fix portfolio reporting

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Carbon emissions metrics have moved from the margins of investment reporting to its centre, driven by regulation, financial risk and rising stakeholder expectations.

According to recent reports, ortec Finance argues that reliable emissions data allows asset owners and managers to assess regulatory, cost and reputational risks, set achievable reduction targets and track progress towards net zero. Looking at both absolute emissions and emissions intensity across all three scopes gives investors a clearer picture of their holdings’ environmental footprints, provided they account for data gaps and inconsistent reporting methodologies.

According to recent reports, yet according to Ortec Finance, headline emissions figures on their own can mislead. Unless reporting links investment decisions to their carbon and financial effects, asset owners cannot tell whether a lower footprint reflects active management, policy exclusions, revised construction rules or simply changes in the underlying data.

The regulatory stakes are significant. Carbon tracking is now a legal requirement in many jurisdictions, with frameworks such as CSRD and SFDR obliging financial institutions to disclose both direct footprints and indirect financed emissions. Failure to comply can bring heavy penalties, litigation and restricted access to major capital markets.

Sources indicate that financially, carbon-intensive portfolios face greater risk of stranded assets and sharp valuation falls, as well as exposure to carbon taxes and rising operating costs. Strong carbon performance, by contrast, is increasingly linked to cheaper borrowing, alpha generation and access to a fast-growing pool of sustainability-focused capital.

Sources indicate that ortec Finance explains that a portfolio carbon footprint combines holdings and weights with emissions data, typically expressed as financed emissions or as a normalised figure such as carbon intensity. In its example, a portfolio invests €1m in Organization A, valued at €100m and emitting 10,000 tonnes of CO2-equivalent a year, and €1m in Company B, valued at €200m and emitting 40,000 tonnes. The attributed emissions are 100 tonnes and 200 tonnes respectively, giving a total of 300 tonnes, or 150 tonnes of CO2-equivalent per €1m invested.

Sources indicate that the firm sets out three essentials for meaningful reporting. First, clear metrics and a consistent benchmark: financed emissions and emissions intensity work best together, but changes over time only mean something against a stable reference point.

Second, decision-level attribution: comparing results with intermediate benchmarks tailored to specific choices isolates the effects of exclusions, constraints, manager allocation and security selection, exposing trade-offs between financial and environmental goals.

In a recent development, third, transparency on data quality: reports should flag gaps, explain estimates and proxies, and separate reported from calculated figures. Ortec Finance cautions that shifts in disclosure coverage or inflation-driven revenue growth can alter intensity metrics without any genuine environmental improvement.

In a recent development, ultimately, Ortec Finance believes carbon reporting delivers most value when it shapes investment decisions rather than merely satisfying disclosure rules. Extending traditional performance attribution to cover emissions alongside returns, risk and allocation and selection effects creates a multi-dimensional view of portfolio outcomes. This ex-post analysis closes the feedback loop, revealing where financial and sustainability goals aligned or clashed, and helping investors build portfolios that balance return, risk and climate objectives.
