What are the best ‘budget premium’ laptops of 2026? It comes down to these 3

🌐 Source: Zdnet • 📅 18 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 7 min read (1444 words, 📚 Deep Dive)
💡 Key Takeaways

  • We’re talking OLED displays, ultralight builds, premium touchpads and keyboards, and most importantly, bold colors that go beyond bland, corporate gray.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Boring colors, dingy displays, and clunky forms — that’s what $699 on a Windows PC used to get you. All that changed with the MacBook Neo, however. Apple’s “budget premium” laptop shook up the market, forcing manufacturers to respond with better laptops in the sub-$1,000 price point.

Also: MacBook Neo to get its most-requested upgrade.

We’re talking OLED displays, ultralight builds, premium touchpads and keyboards, and most importantly, bold colors that go beyond bland, corporate gray. With all the major PC brands releasing their own models, there are plenty of options in the budget range, and as memory costs continue to skyrocket, the timing couldn’t be better.

But how do they perform? Well, they’re certainly running lighter hardware, but if you’re aware of their limitations, this may not be a bad thing. For students and kids, for example, they’re solid contenders for surfing the web, running Office 365, and working out of Google Docs. Ultimately, these laptops offer a ton of bang for their buck. Here are the top three I recommend.

HP’s 2026 OmniBook 5 is a solid budget premium laptop with a lightweight, lovely build and a very attractive $649 starting price. Available in metallic pink, blue, green, and silver, it’s designed specifically to counter the MacBook Neo’s trade-offs in a thin, lightweight chassis and gorgeous OLED touchscreen display.

Sources indicate that at 2.56 pounds, it’s lighter and thinner than the Neo, at just 11.7mm thick when closed. It’s not a convertible, but the screen does lay completely flat, and HP’s hinge construction is solid, with almost no wobble while typing. The laptop’s physical build doesn’t feel cheap, but there is a small amount of flex and bend, particularly on the bottom plate.

The 14-inch, 2K touchscreen OLED display is the first thing you notice, and the standout feature by far. For a $649 laptop, it offers vivid contrast and good color separation, but it’s a rather glossy display, so you’ll probably find yourself repositioning it as I did during my testing.

Sources indicate that the OmniBook 5 also addresses two other of the Neo’s biggest drawbacks: the lack of an HDMI port and keyboard backlighting, both of which are standard. It runs the Intel Core 5 320 processor, a battery-efficient chip that gives the laptop exceptional runtime — easily 10 hours of consistent use — sufficient for long days on campus if you forget your charger.

Industry observers note that the $649 starting configuration comes with modest hardware: 8GB of memory and a 256GB SSD. That amount of memory all but relegates the laptop to everyday basic tasks like checking email, using Microsoft 365 apps, surfing the web, or writing papers. For students, kids, and teens looking for a laptop for school, that’s the name of the game. But if you’re a professional looking for more processing power, you may need to keep looking.

In a recent development, you can opt for 16GB of memory on the OmniBook 5, but it’ll bring the price up to $899, which just isn’t as competitive. Still, even though Windows 11 performs a little better on 8GB of RAM these days, I wouldn’t recommend opting for that amount of onboard memory and storage unless you’re confident these everyday tasks meet your expected use case.

As mentioned above, students, kids, and teens will gravitate toward the OmniBook’s cool colors and lightweight design. Opting for 16GB will bring the price up, but it’ll also make it a much more well-rounded laptop, opening up use cases in photo and video editing, even gaming.

Sources indicate that dell’s 14S is particularly well-suited for students with Dell’s competitive student pricing strategy. The 14S has the same Intel Core 5 320 processor as the HP OmniBook 5, but it also offers more configurations that, when paired with the student discount, don’t amount to a whole lot more cash. For example, you can get the 8GB model with 512GB of storage for $649, $100 off the regular price — a better deal than the OmniBook 5.

Recent findings suggest that the Dell 14S also has one fantastic battery — lasting over 10 hours in my testing. For students who need something for the entire day on campus, the 14S delivers. It feels a whole lot like its premium cousin, the XPS 13, a fantastic laptop released earlier this year that received ZDNET’s Editors’ Choice award for its premium build and bang-for-buck. The 14S draws on that laptop’s premium construction and solid build.

It has been revealed that it has the same port setup as the OmniBook 5: an HDMI, two USB-Cs, and an audio jack. In terms of the keyboard and trackpad, I found the 14S to have a very “hard” click that’s relatively loud, which might be noticeable in a quiet classroom.

The 14S has the same considerations as the OmniBook, given its modest hardware. In my testing of the 14S with 8GB of RAM, for example, I found performance to be fine for everyday tasks, but there were inherent limits to processing power once a certain number of tabs were open in the browser or several apps were open at once.

Recent findings suggest that the 14S also comes with a non-touch FHD+ (1920×1200) IPS display that’s not as vivid as the OmniBook 5’s OLED or the MacBook Neo’s Liquid Retina. It also doesn’t come with a keyboard backlight standard, a feature the OmniBook 5 flaunts. However, I will say it’s brighter than both of the other two displays at 400 nits, and goes up to a 120Hz refresh rate.

It has been revealed that also, this laptop does have several configurations available, but the model with 16GB of RAM is priced at $1,199, much higher than the $899 OmniBook 5 with 16GB. The build quality is nice, but once you cross the $1,000 threshold, you’re opening yourself up to comparisons with Windows PCs with much more powerful specs.

Industry observers note that students should consider the Dell 14S first and foremost, since the education discount makes higher configurations especially compelling. For everyday owners who work out of the browser and stay within the typical lineup of Microsoft productivity apps, performance is sufficiently snappy, as long as you’re using it for these kinds of tasks.

Industry observers note that the MacBook Neo started this whole conversation, bringing a gorgeous Liquid Retina display, Apple’s signature quality touchpad and keyboard, and exciting colors. Even better, it’s got great battery life thanks to Apple’s A18 Pro chip, and is as portable as you can ask for in a 13-inch model. The Neo succeeded because it did much of what its older siblings can do in MacOS — all the iPhone integrations like FaceTime, Messages, and iPhone Mirroring, with support for one external display up to 4K at 60Hz via USB-C.

The trade-offs come in performance and build, but Apple’s choices here are smart. It foregoes keyboard backlighting, but as a work or school device, it’s not a huge deal. It skips the haptic touchpad, but Apple already makes a good mechanical one. The A18 Pro chip scales back multi-core power, but single-core performance (for the stuff you’re actually doing on the Neo) is great.

All of this for $699 (or $599 with student pricing), earning ZDNET’s Editors’ Choice award for being the complete package laptop for an unheard-of price (it was $599 when it launched, before a subsequent price hike).

In a recent development, unlike the other two laptops on this list, the MacBook Neo is locked into two configurations, each featuring 8GB of unified memory. Granted, 8GB on MacOS goes a lot further than on Windows 11, but still, you’re looking at either 256GB or 512GB of local storage, the former of which is too limiting in my opinion.

Recent findings suggest that as Apple’s first and only device targeting the budget laptop market, the MacBook Neo is also a first-generation product that will undoubtedly have some build and performance improvements in its upcoming generation. As it stands, it’s unupgradeable and locked into a modest hardware configuration. How many years into the future it will continue to serve you is an open question.

The MacBook Neo serves as an excellent entry point into Apple’s ecosystem, especially as MacBook Pros continue to get more and more expensive. For anyone with an iPhone, it’s an easy companion device. The Neo is easily a main driver for students and pros alike, with excellent single-core performance and the versatility to tackle most tasks.
