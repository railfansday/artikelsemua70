Breaking: The domino effect threatening CRS and CARF compliance

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sources indicate that for years, reporting season meant a fixed deadline. Financial institutions filed their forms, checked the figures and put the process aside until the following year. That view no longer reflects reality for most firms.

According to TAINA Technology, regulation has shifted, but the bigger change is a growing awareness that minor weaknesses in how data is collected, maintained and governed can distort reporting outcomes months down the line.

According to recent reports, this year’s season lands amid considerable upheaval. CRS 2.0 brings fresh reporting requirements and extra data points within reporting schemas, while CARF introduces an entirely new regime covering crypto-assets. Local implementation adds further complexity. The UK, for instance, has introduced domestic CARF reporting, an obligation with no equivalent under CRS.

Sources indicate that together, these developments are driving up the volume, complexity and scrutiny of reporting data. Firms are no longer simply expected to produce reports. They must prove that the information underpinning them is complete, accurate and able to support shifting compliance demands.

Questions about CRS 2.0 readiness are now being aired openly, with fund administrators being challenged at industry conferences on their preparedness. The debate has moved beyond filing to whether the data, controls and governance behind each submission are genuinely robust.

Reporting failures seldom begin in the reporting process itself. A minor upstream lapse can snowball into a serious downstream problem. A change in tax residency may never reach the right team.

Sources indicate that A controlling person upgrade might be logged in one system but not another. A self-certification could be accepted yet never make it into reporting data. Each seems trivial alone, but reporting season tends to expose every weak link.

In a recent development, by the time reporting files are assembled, these gaps can see accounts reported to the wrong jurisdiction, wrongly reported to several jurisdictions, or left out entirely. Under-reporting risks regulatory scrutiny, follow-up enquiries and penalties.

Over-reporting carries its own dangers, including data protection breaches where personal information reaches tax authorities that should never have received it, along with reputational damage when errors surface.

Sources indicate that the heaviest cost is often uncertainty rather than any single fine. Once an error is found, firms must establish whether it is isolated or symptomatic. If one account was misreported, how many others were affected by the same flawed process? That question frequently sparks large-scale remediation, renewed client outreach and substantial manual work to restore confidence in the data.

Industry observers note that the takeaway is clear: reporting season really begins at onboarding. Receiving the correct information does not guarantee the correct outcome, as data passes through multiple teams, systems and service providers.

Sources indicate that as firms prepare for CRS 2.0 and CARF, the priority should be data readiness, not merely reporting readiness. Those that maintain data quality and governance year-round will be far better placed to file with confidence, stopping small issues before the dominoes start to fall.
