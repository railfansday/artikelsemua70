Is there need for regulation focused on AI use within wealth management?

🌐 Source: Fintech • 📅 19 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 8 min read (1573 words, 📚 Deep Dive)
💡 Key Takeaways
  • Regulators around the world are steadily bringing artificial intelligence under their watchful eye, but should there be a specific regulation that is focused on AI within wealth management?
  • Traditionally, regulators have been slow to respond to new innovations entering the market.
  • AI has already become well established across the wealth management sector.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Regulators around the world are steadily bringing artificial intelligence under their watchful eye, but should there be a specific regulation that is focused on AI within wealth management?

Traditionally, regulators have been slow to respond to new innovations entering the market. However, AI is a different kettle of fish. Given the pace of change around AI, and its growing impact on the way businesses operate, regulators have been taking a more active approach to regulating the technology. The most notable is the EU AI Act, which is the world’s first regulation entirely focused on AI and establishes a risk-based AI classification system. Other regulators are either looking at building their own frameworks or strengthening existing regulations to encompass AI.

AI has already become well established across the wealth management sector. A recent report from EY found that 95% of firms have scaled their use of generative AI applications across multiple use cases. It also found that 78% of firms are exploring agentic AI to unlock even greater enhancements to their operations.

The entire wealth management value chain is incorporating AI, ranging from back-office functions like compliance and risk management, through to middle-office workflows like investment research and front-office functions, including personalised investment strategies and client engagement.

Given the penetration of AI within wealth management, it could be inevitable that wealth management-specific regulations will start to take shape. Friedhelm Schmitt, co-founder and CEO of Fincite explained, “It will take over more and more of the work right now performed by people in wealth management. I don’t see any realistic scenario in which that doesn’t happen.

In a recent development, “Now, if an AI system can perform a task to the same regulatory standards as a human adviser, why should we need an entirely different set of rules? In principle, we shouldn’t. But there is a practical problem. AI isn’t human. And virtually all our financial regulation was written with human beings in mind.”.

People read rules, interpret them and apply professional judgement. If AI is to be used safely within wealth management systems, regulations need to be designed in a way that machines can understand and adhere to, he explained.

Industry observers note that “I am a strong believer in the idea of Law as Code. We should translate regulatory requirements, wherever possible, into machine-readable and executable rules. Hard, deterministic guardrails that define what an AI system is permitted to do.

In a recent development, “Interestingly, the UK’s Financial Conduct Authority has already experimented with machine-executable regulation alongside major banks. Those early projects focused on regulatory reporting, but I think the underlying idea has much broader implications. Imagine a world where regulatory compliance can be checked continuously, as part of the system itself.

“I would argue that this could make financial services considerably safer than relying primarily on manual controls and retrospective audits.”.

On the other hand, Fredrik Davéus, CEO and co-founder of Kidbrooke believes the core obligations for AI’s use within wealth management are already established. He explained, “The right core obligations are already there. A firm giving investment advice must make sure that the advice is suitable, treat customers fairly and be able to explain itself to the regulator.

Recent findings suggest that “None of that changes because a model drafted the recommendation. In the UK, the FCA has been consistent that it will rely on Consumer Duty and the Senior Managers and Certification Regime instead of writing a separate AI rulebook, and I think that is broadly the right instinct.”.

He also emphasised that while the EU has debuted its AI Act, investment advice is not a use case that it deems high-risk, while credit scoring and life and health insurance pricing are.

However, Davéus does believe there are some gaps where additional regulations could be welcomed within wealth management. “Where existing rules do fall short is in quiet assumptions, in that a firm can reconstruct why a recommendation was made. Traditional financial models are deterministic. Run them twice with the same inputs and you get the same answer. Large language models are probabilistic, and the same question asked twice can produce two different responses.”.

To solve this issue, Davéus would like to see targeted, practical additions that are worked into existing regimes, rather than a fresh, parallel regime.

What would an AI regulation for wealth management look like?

If a regulator were to build a specific regulation for AI use with wealth management, what should it include?

Industry observers note that for Schmitt, there are hundreds of points that could be included in the regulation, covering transparency, explainability, model governance, data quality and much more. However, he believes the best starting point would be to ask, who is responsible?

It has been revealed that he mentioned, “When an AI system gives unsuitable financial advice, makes a bad investment decision or causes financial harm, somebody has to be accountable. And I mean genuinely accountable. A bank shouldn’t be able to point to its technology provider, while the technology provider points to the model developer, and everyone ends up explaining why the decision wasn’t really theirs. We need a clear answer to that question before we go much further.”.

In a recent development, if a wealth management firm decides to leverage AI, it must remain accountable to clients, he stated. As such, how responsibility is allocated between the institutions and technology will need to be clearly defined. If this is not done correctly, it risks fostering an environment where key financial decisions are made without anyone taking responsibility for them. “I don’t think customers, regulators or the industry itself can afford that.”.

As for Davéus, he pointed to five crucial principles that are essential for a specific AI regulation. At the top of that list is a clear line between language and numbers. Any figure provided to a client, whether it is a projected pension, risk score or probability of reaching a goal, should be from a validated, auditable model. It should never come from a language model. He remarked, “AI is excellent at explaining, summarising and holding a conversation. It should never be the calculator.”.

Second is reproducibility and record-keeping. Firms should be able to replay an AI-assisted recommendation with the same inputs, model version, assumptions and data and be provided with the same result. “If you cannot reconstruct it, you cannot defend it to a client, an ombudsman or a regulator.”.

Third, an AI tool that summarises meeting notes for an adviser carries very different risk to one that is used to directly recommend portfolios to customers. The rules should account for this distinction, so firms do not need to apply their heaviest controls to the lowest-risk tasks.

In a recent development, the fourth area is keeping accountability internal. While most firms will leverage models that were built by a third-party, the regulated party should be expected to understand, test and monitor the model.

In a recent development, finally, Davéus points to the need for outcome testing. Firms should monitor results across various client groups to identify instances of bias or poor outcomes. Regulators, meanwhile, should support this through sandboxes and testing.

Would regulation impact innovation?

When it comes to regulation some that consider it a barrier to innovation and others that consider it a necessary support system.

Recent findings suggest that for Davéus, it all depends on how the regulation is written. He noted, “Today the biggest brake on AI in wealth management is uncertainty. I speak to firms with promising pilots sitting in internal review because nobody can say with confidence what their compliance team, or the regulator, will accept once the tool faces clients. A clear set of expectations would let those firms commit budget and move.”.

Unfortunately, if the rules are too prescriptive, they will have the opposite effect. “Any regulation that names specific technologies or model types will be out of date before it comes into force. Europe’s own experience is a useful warning, having already had to postpone its high-risk AI obligations while the standards caught up. The best outcome is a set of principles backed by concrete technical expectations on traceability, testing and accountability.”.

Schmitt also sees financial regulation as a “paradox in financial regulation.” He noted that while rules are implemented to protect smaller investors, those rules are often so complicated and expensive to comply with that only the largest institutions can afford to operate under them.

According to recent reports, “A large bank can hire another hundred people for compliance. A small technology company cannot.

“The result is that we sometimes end up protecting the very institutions we wanted to regulate, simply because we have made it so difficult for anyone else to compete.”.

This is a scenario that Schmitt is worried about happening to AI. The technology is capable of making high-quality advice accessible to millions of people who were previously unable to afford it, helping to reduce the wealth gap.

“It would be rather ironic if we then regulated that technology in a way that made it economically viable only for the largest banks.”.

He concluded, “I want strong consumer protection. Absolutely. But I also want a market where upcoming companies can challenge established institutions and where people benefit from better and more affordable financial services. If we design AI regulation well, we can have both. And I think regulators should be very conscious of that responsibility.”.
