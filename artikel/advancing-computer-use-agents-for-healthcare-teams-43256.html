Advancing computer-use agents for healthcare teams

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Healthcare is fragmented, and teams often need to move information between systems manually before a patient can get care. When a clinician recommends an MRI, for example, the information an insurer needs may be in a patient’s medical record, but a team member still needs to retrieve it and enter it into a separate portal to complete an authorization request.

Recent findings suggest that computer-use agents carry out multi-step workflows by clicking, typing, and moving between applications. In healthcare, this enables AI to help with repetitive workflows in the software teams already use.

Industry observers note that today, we’re sharing upcoming results measuring model performance, safety, and cost in clinical and administrative workflows. We’re also sharing the ways we’re working with leading healthcare organizations like Abridge, Ambience, Cigna Healthcare, and CVS Health to advance and apply computer use, across authorization requests, referrals, discharge planning, and outreach between visits.

Industry observers note that healthcare teams, developers, and individual clinicians can access these capabilities today through the OpenAI API, ChatGPT for Healthcare, and ChatGPT for Clinicians. Organizations can support HIPAA-compliant workflows, configure approvals and agent access, and record audit logs for secure deployments.

To show how a computer-use agent can help a clinician and an authorization specialist work on the same MRI request, we built a demo using synthetic patient data. A patient messages their clinician about lower back pain. A computer-use agent reviews the patient’s visit notes, physical therapy records, and prior imaging in the EHR. It drafts an MRI order, an encounter note, and a reply to the patient for the clinician to review. The agent then uses the same records to prepare a prior authorization request in the insurer’s portal for an authorization specialist to review.

It has been revealed that we evaluated GPT‑6 Astra on tasks clinicians handle in their EHR inboxes. These tasks, shaped by physician input, require Astra to carry out multi-step workflows designed to test both clinical reasoning over longitudinal patient context and navigation of complex interfaces. Reviewers examined the model’s actions alongside the notes, orders, and patient messages it produced, assessing whether the work would be helpful and clinically safe in the context of the task.

Clinical safety was assessed by evaluating whether inaccuracies, omissions, or actions in the model’s work could plausibly lead to patient harm. Overall, 99% of 325 reviewed tasks were rated safe. Physicians rated helpfulness on a five-point scale, selecting “very helpful” or “extremely helpful” in 93% of 646 assessments.

We also evaluated computer-use agents for administrative tasks, such as prior authorizations, appeals, and medical equipment orders. On HealthAdminBench⁠(opens in a fresh window), an independent benchmark from Stanford researchers measuring performance on common administrative workflows, GPT‑6 Astra earned 94.3% of available rubric points, while GPT‑6 Luna earned 91.8%, despite costing 98% less at 8.5 cents per task. Both models were evaluated at maximum reasoning effort. These results show progress toward capable, affordable agents for high-volume administrative workflows.

Our partners build software used in hundreds of health systems, coordinate care for members globally, and deliver care to hundreds of millions of patients. The following examples showcase how organizations are developing, piloting, and deploying computer-use applications to save time, solve critical problems, and improve patient care.

Recent findings suggest that CVS Health is using OpenAI’s computer-use capabilities to automate repetitive work across clinical systems, including sending documents, processing referrals, and supporting specialist consults. The agents navigate existing applications to complete steps that APIs do not fully support.

In one production workflow, an agent cleared a months-long document-sending backlog over a weekend. Another handles the system steps for tens of thousands of consults each day, helping team members focus on work that needs their expertise. CVS Health uses validation checks and human oversight, with screenshots and action records to verify actions and application state. CVS Health is open sourcing its Agent Kit⁠(opens in a fresh window), built with OpenAI’s Agents SDK, which packages this approach as a repeatable process for broader use.

It has been revealed that data remains within the CVS Health enterprise-controlled infrastructure, governed by their enterprise AI Governance team and established responsible AI policies. Production data is not used to train foundation models.

Sources indicate that abridge is developing computer-use agents to help clinicians carry out care aims across use cases and applications they use daily, including those without APIs. Their planned inpatient use case builds on the clinical conversation, adding EHR information and nurse and clinician context to identify what needs attention and to coordinate next steps.

For example, a clinician can ask the Abridge Assistant to help resolve a patient’s discharge barriers. Agents can work concurrently on medication access and home-health coordination, using computer interaction alongside calls and messages. The clinician stays focused on the patient and is brought in for decisions requiring clinical judgment.

Sources indicate that as part of The Cigna Group’s broader collaboration with OpenAI, Cigna Healthcare is using OpenAI’s frontier models alongside its own clinical expertise to bring together relevant information across systems and give care teams more complete context about each member’s needs and experience. It has been revealed that computer use lets agents complete tasks in EHRs and payer portals that APIs don’t reach. In healthcare, that capability is only usable if every action is controlled and auditable. Ambience built additional controls to support that oversight.

In a recent development, in this demonstration, Ambience uses evidence from the patient’s chart to prepare a prior-authorization request on an insurer’s website. When the record is ambiguous, the agent stops and asks the clinician, then waits for approval before submitting. Health systems set which actions the agent can take, can pause it at any point, and get a step-by-step log for audit.

Computer-use agents in healthcare require evaluating clinical safety, protecting patient information, and setting clear limits on what agents can do without human review.
