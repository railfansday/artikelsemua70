Why trade-based money laundering threatens insurers now

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Trade-based money laundering (TBML) remains one of the most resilient and elusive ways criminals exploit financial services. While banks have long faced scrutiny over the practice, trade credit insurers are increasingly exposed and have largely stayed out of the conversation.

According to RelyComply, this is partly because global anti-money laundering (AML) guidance has seldom examined the link between the two.

It has been revealed that in South Africa, the Financial Intelligence Centre Act (FICA) is prompting questions about insurers’ accountability, particularly where trade-based business is concerned. TBML has been tied to the trafficking of drugs, arms, tobacco and wildlife, and with undetected fraud thought to outnumber detected cases tenfold, the sector’s historic silence on financial crime is becoming a serious vulnerability.

Part of the difficulty lies in sheer scale. International trade in goods and commercial services reached $34.89tn in 2025, and value moves through physical goods as well as payments.

Criminals rely on over- and under-invoicing, where the true value of goods or services is misstated and illicit funds cover the difference, an approach that requires collusion between importer and exporter.

Industry observers note that other tactics include billing multiple times for the same shipment to exploit jurisdictional gaps, and “ghost shipments” of goods that never exist. The UK’s National Risk Assessment 2025 also flags trade in services, where values and descriptions are easily manipulated, while the Financial Action Task Force (FATF) and the Egmont Group highlight goods with wide pricing margins as a common risk.

Detection falls between the cracks. Banks see the money but not the goods, while customs officials see the goods but focus on contraband and tariffs. Trade credit insurers, however, sit directly within the transaction flow. Their role is to protect manufacturers, suppliers and traders against buyers who pay late or become insolvent, paying out a share of outstanding invoice debt.

The International Credit Insurance & Surety Association (ICISA) describes this cover as fundamental to trade. Yet it also means insurers may be underwriting invoices that criminal networks use to move dirty money.

The problem is that due diligence typically stops once an insurer confirms a counterparty can pay. Whether the transactions themselves are legitimate goes unchecked.

Ongoing solvency monitoring already resembles perpetual know your customer (pKYC) processes, but insurers often lack the tools to act on financial crime signals, including awareness of TBML typologies, trade flow monitoring and beneficial ownership checks to uncover shell companies and sanctioned parties.

FATF and the Egmont Group’s 2021 risk indicators offer a starting point. Insurers can strengthen defences by verifying policyholders and their buyers, building centralised teams that combine AML and fraud expertise, and adopting automated platforms with identity verification, dynamic risk scoring and enhanced due diligence. RegTech partners can help introduce sector-specific tools before regulation demands them.

For South African insurers, the message is clear: financial crime detection must now sit alongside credit risk assessment, before tighter rules force a rushed overhaul.
