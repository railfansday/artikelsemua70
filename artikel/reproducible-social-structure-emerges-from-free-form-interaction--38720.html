Reproducible Social Structure Emerges From Free-Form Interaction Among LLM Agents

🌐 Source: Arxiv • 📅 26 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 1 min read (273 words, 📖 Standard)

arXiv:2611.31543v1 Announce Type: cross Abstract: Multi-agent LLM simulations are increasingly used as models of social systems, but most published findings are anecdotal, i.e., drawn from a single run. It is therefore unclear which observed phenomena are robust findings of the simulated system itself, and which were random events in one particular run. Here, we introduce a repeatable, goal-free, proactive multi-agent framework in which fifty persona-conditioned agents with persistent memory choose whether, with whom, and how to communicate across three social platforms. The framework supports non-perturbative measurements across temporal snapshots and repetitions. Running the same world ten times from identical initial conditions for a total of 700 hours, we find that most measured network- and agent-level parameters are stable across repetitions. Throughout a run, initially broad communication contracts into sparse sustained dyads in all ten worlds. Importantly, we find that many identical dyads re-form across worlds significantly more often than expected by chance or from agent-specific sociability alone. Static personality similarity largely fails to predict these dyads. Instead, alignment and extremity in the emotions and traits expressed during first contact predict whether a pair later forms a sustained dyad. Additionally, individual agents' emotion and trait expression in their chain-of-thought predicts their future behavior. Finally, swapping either persona prompts or accumulated memories between agents causes recipients to exhibit behavior more similar to donors. Both kinds of transplant move the recipients about 60% of the way toward its donor personality, and state-transplanted agents start behaving similar to their donor agent from their next turn on. Overall, we show that repeated worlds enable robust, statistically-grounded conclusions about emergent within-configuration behavior in agent societies.
