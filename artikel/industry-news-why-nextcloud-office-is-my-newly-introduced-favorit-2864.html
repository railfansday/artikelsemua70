The office suite is something we all take for granted. It’s there. We depend on it. It works. Not until we realize there are better options available do we wonder why we’ve stuck with our default for so long.

It has been revealed that in June, a team of European developers debuted a newly introduced office suite called Euro-Office, billed as an open-source alternative to Microsoft Office and Google Docs. At launch, Euro-Office was web-only suite that included typical tools for word processing, spreadsheets, presentations, and PDFs.

Recently, however, Nextcloud developers released a desktop version of the app called Nextcloud Office Powered by Euro-Office — Nextcloud Office, for short.

The desktop app can be installed on Linux, MacOS, and Windows. (Make sure you’ve downloaded the installer that is named “Nextcloud-Office-DesktopEditors”; otherwise, the MacOS version will not run due to a missing code signature.) When you open the app, it should be called Nextcloud Office (as opposed to Euro-Office — there are several versions floating around, and I’ve installed both).

The one thing missing from Nextcloud Office is any sort of AI integration, so if that’s a must-have for you, look elsewhere.

Industry observers note that with so many office suites to choose from, that’s the massive question. One of my reasons for choosing Nextcloud Office is because I can use it either locally or connected to a cloud instance. I can take that one step further and connect the Nextcloud Office desktop app to a locally installed instance of Nextcloud.

In other words, I get the best of both worlds: a cloud instance that doesn’t go outside of my LAN. By doing this, I can access all of my documents from any machine on my network, without worrying that my data is being seen or used by a third-party organization.

You don’t have to install an instance of Nextcloud on your LAN; you can simply use Nextcloud Office as a traditional suite, where your files are saved on your machine and nowhere else (unless you save them on a shared drive on your network).

That’s part of the beauty of Nextcloud Office; it gives you options that most office suites don’t.

It has been revealed that does this all sound familiar? That’s because Nextcloud Office is a fork of another of my favorite office suites, OnlyOffice Desktop Editors.

Beyond the whole local/cloud feature, what else does Nextcloud Office offer? As noted earlier, it has all of the bits you need for a standard office suite, including templates, collaboration tools (track changes, comments, compare, and combine), multi-page view, annotations, document protection, and a well-designed layout.

According to recent reports, because I have extensive experience with the word processor portion of an office suite, my testing focused on that. I’ll continue to test the other areas of Nextcloud Office (and report later), but for this comparison, I stuck with the word processor — Nextcloud Write.

To make a comparison between Nextcloud Write and other top office suite word processors (Word, LibreOffice, Apple Pages), I opened one of my manuscripts with tons of track changes and comments to see how it would fare.

Sources indicate that the collaboration tools worked fine in MS Word. In LibreOffice Writer, the comments and track changes follow a different — and fairly cumbersome — method. I’ve always liked the way Apple Pages handles comments and track changes, but I no longer trust the app with my work (it will randomly fail to write to a file).

When it came time to open the same file in Nextcloud, I had my doubts, given how fresh this app is. To my surprise, Nextcloud handled the comments and track changes very similarly to that of MS Word (the main difference is that MS Word places the comments sidebar on the right, whereas Nextcloud Write places it on the left).

I was also pleased to see that Nextcloud Office document component didn’t get bogged down when handling a 100+ page document with hundreds of comments and track changes. In fact, it performed as well with that document as it did with a 10-pager containing zero comments or track changes.

I did want to at least check to see how well Nextcloud Office would handle a spreadsheet that was designed in Excel. To that end, I downloaded various templates from Office 365 to see how well Nextcloud Spreadsheet would handle them. Other than some font challenges (because I didn’t have certain fonts initially installed on Linux), every Excel template I opened worked fine.

As I continued using Nextcloud Office, I rapidly realized that I have zero complaints about the app. It’s fast, stable, opens all of the documents I need, has all of the features you would find in the competition, it’s open-source, and it’s free. I will continue to work with the other aspects of Nextcloud Office and report back on my findings at a later date.

In a recent development, if you’ve been on the hunt for an office suite to replace MS Office, LibreOffice, or Apple Pages, trust me when I tell you that Nextcloud Office is the one. I knew, the second I opened it, that it would become my fresh default office suite on my two operating systems of choice: Linux and MacOS.
