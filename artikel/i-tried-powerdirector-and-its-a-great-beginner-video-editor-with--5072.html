In a recent development, cyberLink’s PowerDirector sits somewhere between other entry- and pro-level video editors. It makes advanced editing accessible to both everyday creators and prosumers while also offering features pros appreciate. Even better, PowerDirector can run on off-the-shelf hardware, so you don’t need to invest in an über-powerful editing machine (as I did with a Mac Studio/M3 Ultra/96GB RAM).

Also: 5 upcoming features in DaVinci Resolve 19 that make my favorite video editor even better.

In a recent development, the manufacturer has invested heavily in AI-driven workflows, including prompt-based editing, generative video tools, and automated editing, to create a full-featured video editor. Although the AI features are cool (more on that in a bit), I want to first focus on the editor itself because that’s the bread and butter of PowerDirector.

Sources indicate that when I first installed PowerDirector, I did so on a Windows 11 virtual machine and had nothing but problems. After switching to my M1 MacBook Pro, it was smooth sailing. Don’t make this mistake.

According to recent reports, the editor is probably the biggest selling point of PowerDirector. One reason for that is its simplicity: Add clips to the library, drag a clip to the timeline, start cutting, add transitions and effects, add overlays, or whatever you need. Although I don’t believe PowerDirector is on the same level as DaVinci Resolve, it does a good job of simplifying tasks that might otherwise be too much for the average user — especially in other, less user-friendly apps.

Also: I was a Final Cut Pro diehard until DaVinci Resolve won me over with these 3 features.

In a recent development, instead of having to create DaVinci Resolve node trees, you simply load your clip, click on, say, Light Adjustment, and move a few sliders to see immediate results. There are no complications with PowerDirector, which means just about anyone can jump in and figure it out. It took me a couple of years to get comfortable enough with DaVinci Resolve to really feel like I knew what I was doing. I jumped into PowerDirector and figured it out in seconds.

It has been revealed that for example, I was able to take Log footage from my ZCAM E2-F6 and fairly quickly color grade it using the simple color sliders. I bumped up the saturation, used the built-in auto white balance tool, and, in seconds, had the clip looking pretty good. I dropped a transition at the beginning and end, and it was ready to export. That took all of two minutes.

Industry observers note that on top of the easy-to-use editor, the app includes a ton of effects, LUTs, transitions, decorations, frames, and other enhancements, so you won’t have to go looking for plugins as you might with Final Cut Pro or DaVinci Resolve. I’ve spent far too much money on plugins for Final Cut Pro and DaVinci Resolve, so trust me when I tell you that having them all built in is a bonus.

Although you’re not going to be producing Netflix-ready videos, if you are a prosumer, you’ll find the PowerDirector editor immediately accessible and understandable, with enough power to give you considerable room to grow. I would even go so far as to say that PowerDirector might be a better first (serious) video editor than Final Cut Pro (and certainly DaVinci Resolve).

That’s certainly an impressive list of features. But does it succeed? Although I didn’t test every feature, I ran through what I consider the more vital tasks to see whether PowerDirector would be a tool I’d add to my digital toolbox.

According to recent reports, one of the AI features that will appeal to most consumers (especially those who don’t have as much experience with editing/coloring) is the AI-based auto-edit feature. With this option, you can tell PowerDirector how you want the clip to look, and it will do its best to match your description. Yes, I could do that manually (which I’d prefer), but if you don’t have the necessary understanding of editing and color grading, this can be a real time-saver.

Industry observers note that another outstanding AI feature is the image-to-video generation. I found this to be a more reliable option than the text-to-video option. All I had to do was upload an image, select a style, and PowerDirector would generate a short clip from the information it was given. Obviously, as with any generative AI, there are limitations.

Recent findings suggest that during my testing, I had PowerDirector create several videos using its built-in AI tools. For the most part, the clips I was able to make were capped at 30 seconds, which didn’t surprise me, as the tool isn’t designed to create feature-length films with AI. Let me show you two particular workflows I tested.

First, I wanted to see what the AI image-to-video feature could do. I decided to use the Horror Movie template and a photo of a cat under a tree. With this task, PowerDirector impressed me. It took that sweet image of a cat under a Christmas tree and turned it into a haunting, 10-second clip of the cat venturing into what looked like an evil passage through the tree into a blood-red abyss. Well played.

In a recent development, it was time to try the AI text-to-video feature. I decided to really push the AI to see what it could do, so I added the following prompt:.

According to recent reports, with the prompt in place, I noticed it would take 266 credits to generate the video. I had enough, so I clicked Generate. Given the complexity of the prompt, I knew it would take some time for the cloud-based AI to generate the video. Yes, you read that correctly: The AI used in PowerDirector isn’t installed on your local machine. The reason is that unless you dropped $10,000 or so on your desktop computer, it most likely doesn’t have the memory to generate AI video.

It has been revealed that the end result wasn’t actually all that bad. No, it wasn’t realistic (especially given that the soldiers on horseback marched directly into the sharpened poles of their foes, which should have speared the poor horses), but this was not bad for AI.

What this final test proved to me was that PowerDirector’s AI does work. I asked it to create some fairly complex videos, and it did a fairly good job.

As with anything AI-related, using it in a video editor requires you to be very specific in your queries. The more specific you are, the better your chances of winding up with a usable video. With PowerDirector, I was able to generate some useful clips that could then be edited together, but only the clips where I offered enough specifics to keep the AI from having to guess were usable.

It has been revealed that is PowerDirector the editor for you? It ultimately depends on what you need a video editor for. If you’re looking for a professional-grade editor that results in narrative videos you can submit to streaming services such as Netflix, keep looking.

If, however, you’re looking for an editor that’s easy to jump into and can create content that can be used for manufacturer videos, interviews, or social media posts, or even help you learn the basics of color grading and editing (without having to dive into the complexity that is DaVinci Resolve), PowerDirector is a great option.
