Recent findings suggest that I missed the Flipper Zero Kickstarter in 2020, and I didn’t get my hands on one until 2023. Since then, it’s been one of those devices that continually exceed my expectations. It’s the perfect gadget for making the unseen world of point-to-point access-control protocols visible, including sub-GHz radio, NFC, 125kHz RFID, infrared, and iButton. The stuff Flipper calls “Layer 0.”.

According to recent reports, just when I thought I’d reached the limits of what the Zero was capable of, I discovered the world of third-party firmware, which opened the door to even more features and tools. Over the years, that scene has grown into an active community of developers building all sorts of novel apps for the platform.

Recent findings suggest that by May 2026, Flipper had sold over a million Zero units, and a large community had gathered around the device. When the tech giant introduced the Flipper One, it made it clear it wasn’t a replacement, but a totally new device. Comparing the two is like comparing a hammer to a screwdriver, so what are the major differences?

The Flipper One is a full Linux computer (running the Debian-based Flipper OS on a mainline Linux kernel) built to work with everything that’s IP-connected: Wi-Fi, Ethernet, and, via M.2 expansion modules, 5G and satellite. If you wanted, you could use the One as a router, a VPN gateway, or a bridge between wired and wireless networks — so-called “layer 1” stuff.

The Zero is one of those rare devices that continually managed to exceed my expectations. It was a small, pocket-sized gadget that made visible the unseen world of point-to-point access-control protocols such as sub-GHz radio, NFC, 125kHz RFID, infrared, and iButton.

I’ve lent my Zero to youngster, and within a few minutes they’re using it to do real stuff, and they’re learning as they go. Everything is built-in, and Flipper the Dolphin (took me a while to get that) is there to guide you through the steps.

You could easily spend six months running the software on the Zero and working your way through the Lab, Flipper’s free app store, before you’re ready to delve into the third-party firmware options out there.

According to recent reports, and thanks to Flipper selling over a million units, that third-party scene has grown into an active community of developers building all sorts of novel apps for the platform. When you’re ready to make that leap, I suggest starting with Unleashed. This will give you the familiarity of the firmware that came with the Flipper Zero, but add features — such as extending the sub-GHz frequency range by removing geolocation blocks and adding a lot of NFC-related features, and fun things like games.

It has been revealed that you can pick up a Zero now for $169 and be using it to explore the world around you by the end of the week. I’m not saying don’t jump on the Kickstarter and get a One for the special price of $399 (especially considering that once it’s out, it’ll retail for $550), but that unit won’t be in your hands until at least July 2027.

The One is a very different beast to the Zero. While the Zero runs FreeRTOS-based firmware, the One is a full computer powered by the Debian-based Flipper OS on a mainline Linux kernel. This means that the One can have USB, HDMI, and Ethernet ports, and like a PC, it’s expandable thanks to the M.2 slot which supports SSD drives and optional radio modules.

You can even connect a One to a keyboard and external display for a full desktop experience.

While the Zero is the tool for point-to-point access-control protocols where there’s no network, no IP addresses, and no internet involved, the One is for everything IP-connected, such as Wi-Fi, Ethernet, and, by making use of optional M.2 modules, 5G, LoRa, and satellite. This means that you can customize the One to be a network appliance such as a router, VPN gateway, or a bridge between wired and wireless networks.

Sources indicate that being a full computer also means the Wi-Fi will be more capable and fully featured than the Wi-Fi on a laptop. Anything that you can do on a computer, you can do with the One.

Flipper’s aim with the One is “to create a fully upstream-supported ARM computer that never becomes outdated.” To achieve this, Flipper is putting its drivers into mainline Linux rather than building a custom kernel. The One should continue to get kernel updates for a long time to come. But as of right now, the software is unfinished, and it has been asking the community to help finish drivers and core software.

It’s worth pointing out that some have their doubts that Flipper will be able to achieve its goal of no binary blobs, no proprietary drivers, and no vendor-locked board support package, and that there will still be some for things like Wi-Fi and cellular connectivity.

According to recent reports, I wish I could simply recommend one over the other, but it’s not that kind of choice. The real question is whether you need “Layer 0” or “Layer 1” tools, not which Flipper is “better”. You might decide you need both. Full disclosure: I haven’t gotten my hands on a One yet, and the question is — can Flipper make it easy to use?

The Zero wasn’t a success because it was powerful, it was a success because it made an invisible world fun and easy to explore for a relatively small price tag and with almost no learning curve.

Also: I’ve owned 50+ portable power stations – one number matters most for its lifespan.

The One has been made to a different script. It’s more than twice the price; it’s bulkier, a full Linux machine with a learning curve to match, and you’ll need modules (and probably an external display) to get the most out of it. If Flipper can make network tinkering as friendly as the Zero made radio, it’ll be a hit. If it can’t… well, the One becomes yet another mini-PC. And this question can’t be answered until the One ships.

One thing’s for sure, I can’t wait to get my hands on it.
