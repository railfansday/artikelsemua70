Flowtica Verso vs. Comulytic Note Pro: This one wins on speed – and price

It has been revealed that we primarily interact with AI via our phones and computers, but there’s a growing subset of devices that act as a go-between. The newly introduced Flowtica Verso is one such device. And it’s got something that’s new to this class of device — a substantial touchscreen display.

Also: The Flowtica Verso AI notetaker is my newly introduced favorite MagSafe gadget.

The Verso is a standalone device, but it also has a MagSafe connector so you can attach it to the back of your smartphone. Yes, that means yet another screen, which can be a lot to ask. I recently tested the Verso to see if it does what it claims, and compared it to Comulytic’s Note Pro, a device I reviewed favorably earlier this year.

I’m old enough to have been taught a bunch of now obsolete skills, shorthand being one. Another skill that’s come and gone is dictation and transcription. I owned several recorders over the years — from tape to digital — and used them extensively.

I use them, however, because a close encounter with a disease-ridden Scottish tick left me with a bit of cramping and neuropathy in my hands, ultimately nudging me toward investigating more non-keyboard options for my phone.

Aside from being potential time-savers, AI voice recorders are a next-generation accessibility solution. After all, we’re all one misfortune away from needing something to make the digital world more accessible.

It has been revealed that I’ve tried quite a few AI voice recorders, and to date my favorite has been the Comulytic Note Pro, which doesn’t have the Verso’s massive e-paper display and currently costs $130. I’ve used this more than I thought I would, but I have to admit that it still hasn’t integrated itself into my workflow in a way that makes it a must-have.

Now that I’ve used both for a while, I think I can offer some grist for the mill for anyone on the fence about whether they want an AI voice recorder with or without an e-paper touchscreen.

Industry observers note that both the Flowtica Verso and Comulytic’s Note Pro are excellent. Despite the Verso having twice as many microphones as the Note Pro (four compared to two, plus a dedicated conduction mic for recording phone calls), I really can’t find a significant difference in recording quality between the two.

In a recent development, the Comulytic Note Pro is smaller, thinner, and lighter, and feels more like a finder card than a recorder. There’s a button to start recording and a tiny 0.8-inch LCD status display to keep you in the loop. And it offers a quite decent battery life of 45 hours (and 107 days on standby). The Note Pro is simple; all you need to worry about is keeping it charged and pressing the button to start recording.

Recent findings suggest that I want to like the Verso. It’s a nice bit of hardware with a premium, durable feel, and it comes packed with the promise of making my life easier. And some aspects of the device work well. Its voice pickup — even when multiple people are talking — is unnervingly accurate, and the transcription is excellent.

It’s also very good at recording phone conversations, though I noticed that using a phone case can reduce accuracy. I’m assuming that this gives the conduction mic, which uses vibration to pick up the other side of phone conversations, less to go on. Battery life is excellent as well, and I have no problem believing Flowtica’s 70-hour runtime claim.

Recent findings suggest that the Verso is slow. Really, painfully slow.

With the Note Pro, you press a button, and off you go. With the Verso, there’s a noticeable lag as you sit there waiting for the screen to refresh and the action to kick off.

E-paper always has a lag, but this goes beyond that. I first found out how frustrating this lag was when I needed to enter a 16-character Wi-Fi passcode. Switching from uppercase to lowercase was tedious, and the delay when switching the keyboard to numeric mode made me think it wasn’t working properly. It was; I just wasn’t giving it time before jabbing some more.

In a recent development, this lag also reduced my ability to read transcriptions on the device. It was quicker, easier, and less frustrating to look at them on my iPhone because using the Verso’s display felt like going back to an old Kindle Paperwhite (in a bad way).

This slowness never got better, and I never got used to it. As a result, it undermines the usefulness of the touchscreen as both a display and a user input method. This is a shame because it’s a really neat feature that might keep me off my phone more, but that half-second delay between tap and reaction was too much for me. That’s the way of it with devices that have to be power-efficient. Processing power is always the first thing to be sacrificed.

Also: I’ve owned 50+ power stations – one number matters most.

Bottom line: I found myself getting frustrated by how slow the Verso was, especially when tapping the screen or pressing the buttons. It all felt too hit-and-miss. The Note Pro might have been just as slow behind the scenes, but there was no display to give me that feedback. Also, everything was done either with a button press or through the app. And since I’ve always got my phone with me, this is no hardship.

It has been revealed that the Verso could also be simplified by removing the “Ask Flowtica” button. I don’t see the point of a separate physical button for a feature I already have on plenty of other devices. And to make things worse, it was painfully slow to use.

I could whip out my phone, open a browser, and type my question — and quite possibly another two or three — and get an answer while Flowtica was still thinking.

Industry observers note that I also received a few “no internet connection” messages with the Verso, and no, my internet connection was fine (I did try it on several Wi-Fi networks, with similar results). Maybe these concerns are specific to my device, or maybe they’ll be resolved with future software updates, but these connectivity dropouts certainly got in the way of using the Verso.

The Note Pro just worked. The one button did what it was supposed to do, and there were never any issues moving recordings from the device to the cloud for transcription.

There’s little point in spending money on an AI voice recorder if you’re not going to use AI to process that raw audio and output it as something useful.

Recent findings suggest that both devices come with unlimited transcription, but both companies put limits on how much of the “advanced” features you can use before you have to put skin in the game and buy a subscription. For the Verso, live on-screen transcription, Voice ID, and summaries are advanced features that are limited to 300 minutes a month for those not on a subscription strategy.

If this feels somewhat confusing — and you’re not alone — the easiest way to think about it is that Comulytic’s free tier includes unlimited basic summaries, while Verso’s main screen feature is metered.

The Flowtica Verso hits the market with a list price of $299. That’s a steep premium to pay for that e-paper display compared to the competition. And it feels even steeper when the lag gets in the way as much as it did.

The Comulytic Note Pro is under $130, which is about as cheap as it gets for a good AI voice recorder. Even the biggest name in the game, the Plaud Note Pro, is only $189.

According to recent reports, if I didn’t feel like I was fighting the lag on almost everything I did with the Verso, I’d stick with it. But it’s frustrating, and it’s far easier to slip back into old habits. It’s possible future updates will streamline the Verso’s UI, but if you’re someone who finds it hard to wait half a second for something to happen, it could feel frustrating and is the reason why I’m sticking with the Note Pro for now.

Finally, be aware that subscriptions come into play on both devices, and that the Verso’s screen offers metered free usage for what it calls “AI minutes” before requiring a monthly payment.
