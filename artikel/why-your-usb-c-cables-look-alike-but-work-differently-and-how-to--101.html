This article was supposed to be about my personal experience with a new docking station — specifically, the Plugable TBT-UDH2, a Thunderbolt 5 dock that is a perfect match for any modern high-end PC.

Industry observers note that instead, I wound up going down a deep rabbit hole, learning more than I ever imagined I would need to know about USB-C cables. I know what you’re thinking. Wasn’t the entire point of USB Type-C to eliminate all the confusion of those earlier USB connectors with all their different shapes and sizes?

Recent findings suggest that well, yes. Unfortunately, the Type-C standard only applies to the physical form factor of the connector, not the data transfer protocol or power capabilities of the cable it’s attached to. Because the 24-pin connector is reversible, you can plug it in either way. But what you can do with a USB-C cable depends on what’s between those two connectors.

I paid for some testing hardware and then evaluated dozens of USB-C cables to figure out which one does what and how you can tell the difference. Along the way, I dredged up some memories about old USB standards and learned a whole lot about Thunderbolt and USB4.

I also discovered the one thing you should do every time you get your hands on a new USB-C cable. Trust me, this information will come in handy someday.

I’ve been using Plugable products for years. The tech giant builds a wide range of products that I’ve found to be reliable and well-designed. A quick scan of my workbench shows several Plugable products: a 2.5 Gbps USB Ethernet adapter, a USB-C enclosure for an M.2 NVMe storage device, and the Thunderbolt 5 dock pictured here.

I’ve now tested this Plugable dock with a half-dozen Windows machines, from entry-level to workstation class, built on Intel, AMD, Qualcomm, and Nvidia platforms. It’s packed with a truly impressive collection of ports, including six USB-C, two USB-A, two HDMI 2.1, and a 2.5 Gbps Ethernet jack. By plugging in one USB-C cable, I can power my laptop (up to 140 W) and turn it into a very well-connected desktop PC, with a 38-inch external display, wired Ethernet, an external mouse, keyboard, and speakers, and several external USB drives.

According to recent reports, it’s been a real workhorse, but over the past few months I found myself occasionally puzzled. All Thunderbolt features worked perfectly when connected to USB4 ports, even though the laptops with those ports aren’t Thunderbolt-certified. On one PC, the video display worked properly when connected to a USB-C port on the left side but acted strangely when using the identical port on the right side. And then there was the challenge of figuring out why two cables that looked exactly the same didn’t act alike.

To get to the bottom of these mysteries, I spoke with Plugable’s founder and Chief Technology Officer, Bernie Thompson, who had the answers I was looking for.

Thunderbolt is a technology that delivers fast data, video, and power over a single cable. To use the Thunderbolt brand, hardware and software makers have to meet a demanding certification process.

Thunderbolt 3, which was developed by Intel and debuted in 2015, was the first technology to really show off USB-C’s potential as a single connection for high-speed data, displays, and power. USB4 is based on Intel’s Thunderbolt protocol specification and is backward compatible with USB 3.2, USB 2.0, and Thunderbolt 3 devices.

A USB4 PC can support USB devices, displays, high-speed storage, and many Thunderbolt peripherals over the same USB-C connection.

Thunderbolt 4 takes USB4 capabilities and establishes a tightly specified, certified minimum feature set. Thunderbolt 5 increases bidirectional bandwidth to 80Gbps, with the ability to hit 120Gbps in display-intensive activities.

There’s a pretty big overlap between USB4 and Thunderbolt technology, which is why every USB4 PC I tested worked just fine with the Thunderbolt 5 dock.

Industry observers note that if you’re like me, you have a drawer filled with USB-C cables you’ve collected over the years. So, how can you tell which is the right one for a specific application?

In a recent development, unless it’s labeled, you can’t. That’s the absurd truth about cables that use the USB-C connector, which can have three very different sets of capabilities:.

I pulled three unlabeled cables from my drawer and tried connecting each to various peripherals, including a display and some external hard drives. Although the cables looked about the same, I had no idea why they behaved so differently.

My ZDNET colleague Adrian Kingsley-Hughes tipped me off to the Treedix USB cable tester, and it helped solve a few mysteries.

I’m reasonably certain that this handsomely braided, thick, white cable cost me a small fortune at the Apple Store a few years ago.

As the tester proved, it can handle the maximum allowed by today’s USB Power Delivery standard: 240 W. For data? Sorry, it only supports USB 2.0. I routinely back up 500 GB of data from my PC to a fast external NVMe drive. Using that expensive USB 2.0 cable, the job would take nearly four hours. Swap in a high-speed USB4 cable like the one shown here, with modern storage devices on both ends, and the backup would be complete in just a few minutes.

At this point I started testing every USB-C cable in that drawer, sorting them into neatly labeled bins: PC charging-only cables; USB 3.2 cables (USB A-to-C and C-to-C), capable of high-speed data transfers; old-fashioned, low-power charging cables (USB A-to-C) good for small devices but not much else; and a small but exclusive stack of high-grade USB4 and Thunderbolt cables.

The Treedix device has a button that displays a few additional details about cables, but I wanted more. So I paid $108.24 for a snazzy digital cable tester from Austria called the BLE CaberQU X-ray. It has a much more detailed display than the Treedix device, as well as a mobile app that connects over Bluetooth and saves the history of devices I’ve tested.

Industry observers note that this little gizmo answers a few questions that the simpler tester can’t, including cable health (e.g., broken pins or shorted connections). With the latest firmware, it can report USB4 v2, PD 3.0/3.1 EPR, active-cable characteristics, Thunderbolt generation, and DisplayPort capabilities. If none of that jargon means anything to you, then this device might be overkill.

It also contains details about a chip included in some USB-C cables called an E-Marker, which electronically identifies the cable’s capabilities to the devices at either end. There is no way to divine the presence of an E-Marker chip from just looking at a cable.

A dock is an unusually demanding application for a USB-C cable, because that one wire can simultaneously be charging a laptop, sending high-resolution video to multiple displays, maintaining a high-speed Ethernet connection, and managing multiple USB peripherals and external storage devices.

As I discovered, choosing the right cable matters: a cable that works flawlessly for charging a laptop or connecting an SSD can fail when a docking station asks it to juggle all those tasks simultaneously.

Industry observers note that as for that concern when I connected the dock to the USB-C port on the right side of the Dell XPS 13 laptop? I went looking for a bad cable, but Plugable’s engineering team pointed me to this support article from Dell, which notes that the laptop might have concerns with the display when the monitor is connected to the USB Type-C port on the right side of the computer. “The issue is intermittent,” the support article notes. Intermittent failure modes are the most annoying to troubleshoot.

Which proves the point that cables matter, as do ports, and firmware, and indeed every link in the chain that carries data from a portable PC to a dock to an external device.

As rabbit holes go, this one was relatively fun to explore. It certainly changed the list of criteria I recommend that you look for in a USB cable.

Sources indicate that and then, follow this rule: When that new cable arrives, keep the packaging, and test it if you have the equipment. Most high-end cables these days are marked with a Thunderbolt logo, a USB4 label, or an indicator of power and data speeds. One reason I recommend Plugable gear is that every USB-C cable they include is clearly labeled. (Zoom in on the image at the top of this post to see an example.).

It has been revealed that if your latest purchase doesn’t have those markings, stick a label on it before you throw it into a drawer. Otherwise, in six months you’re going to pull it out of that drawer and wonder what exactly it’s capable of. Don’t go down that rabbit hole.
