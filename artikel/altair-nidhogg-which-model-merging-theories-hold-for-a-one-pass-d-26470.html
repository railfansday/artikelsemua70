Altair-Nidhogg: Which Model-Merging Theories Hold for a One-Pass Decision Encoder? Soups, Task Arithmetic, TIES and DARE Against Joint Training and Prediction Ensembling

🌐 Source: Arxiv • 📅 23 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 1 min read (272 words, 📖 Standard)

arXiv:2611.27550v1 Announce Type: new Abstract: Laya is a small model that answers a multiple-choice question in one pass: each option has a name and a written description. A new skill costs a training run, so we test whether published recipes for merging fine-tuned models (weight averaging, task arithmetic, TIES, DARE) work for it, and whether the theories behind them hold. All 156 models start from one released checkpoint: four plain fine-tunes, five specialists (each adds one kind of training row), joint fine-tunes on all rows, and prediction ensembles. Items are held-out, synthetic and LLM-labelled. In-family, each skill is scored on new items of its own kind, and "kept" is the share of a specialist's gain over the plain fine-tune. Averaging four specialists keeps 52% [38, 64] with +1.7 points of general accuracy; adding the full vectors keeps 42% [3, 63] with -9.0; TIES, which trims and resolves sign conflicts, keeps 64% [50, 76] with +1.5; joint fine-tunes, which see all the rows, keep 120% [104, 162] and 116% [104, 149]. DARE does not repair the full sum (-0.4 points), and ensembles of the models' predictions keep 26% to 54% depending on how they are combined. Swapping the seed of one specialist moves the average by up to 8 points (23 for the full sum). Of 10 predictions fixed in advance, 1 held, 1 held partly, 7 failed and 1 could not be tested. Out-of-family (benchmark questions, code-mixed tickets), the four-way average, joint models and ensemble stay within 2.6 points of the plain fine-tune, but full-vector sums lose up to 7.7. Labels are LLM-made, data synthetic, one base model.
