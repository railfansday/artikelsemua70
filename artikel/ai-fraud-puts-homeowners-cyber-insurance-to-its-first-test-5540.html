AI fraud puts homeowners cyber insurance to its first test

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Personal cyber cover has become a fixture of US homeowners policies, and AI-powered fraud is now testing whether its pricing and terms can hold.

New analysis from ZestyAI, a provider of a risk decision platform for property and casualty insurers, demonstrates carriers are adding restrictions and reconsidering pricing after ten years of steady growth in the product.

The report, titled Cyber Moves Into the Home, examines US homeowners insurance regulatory filings between 2016 and 2026, including filings for standalone personal cyber products.

Recent findings suggest that zestyAI compiled the findings using ZORRO Discover, its competitive intelligence platform. The study traces how the cover grew from a specialist add-on into a widely offered endorsement protecting against six main threats: cyber attacks, cyber extortion, online fraud, data breaches, cyberbullying and identity recovery.

Recent filings point to a change in strategy. Rather than lifting rates on current policies, insurers are launching latest products with higher prices and amended terms. War and cyber-war exclusions show this shift most clearly.

Recent findings suggest that these clauses appeared in only 5% of personal cyber programmes before 2023 but feature in 71% in 2026, the first broad tightening after seven years of expansion. State regulators have reacted inconsistently. Texas and Virginia approved the exclusions while Latest York and North Carolina turned them down, leaving policyholders with different protection depending on where they live.

It has been revealed that the threat is growing rapidly. FBI figures referenced in the study put reported US cybercrime losses at $20.9bn in 2025, a 26% rise on the previous year. Federal Trade Commission data reveals imposter fraud losses hit $3.5bn, close to triple the 2020 figure, as voice cloning and other AI tools make impersonation schemes more convincing.

According to recent reports, pricing, however, has stayed almost flat for eight years. A typical yearly premium sits at about $55, and entry-level charges usually fall between $30 and $75. That band has held since 2018, even though insurers have widened the endorsement and raised maximum limits from $50,000 at launch to as much as $2m.

The report also finds that personal cyber cover reached uniformity more promptly than any modern personal-lines product. Most programmes rely on common policy forms supported by reinsurers rather than wording each carrier builds alone. This allowed rapid rollout, but it also means amendments to wording and exclusions tend to move through the market in waves.

Carriers are also writing more precise terms for social-engineering fraud, where criminals pose as trusted people or organisations to trick victims into sending money. Existing online-fraud clauses usually cover these losses, but newer policies increasingly define and price them as a separate risk.

ZestyAI founder and CEO Attila Toth said, “Personal cyber went from a niche endorsement to a standard part of the homeowners policy in under a decade, at a price that has barely moved since 2018. What is happening now is AI is changing the risk itself faster than any product cycle this industry has experienced before.

“We are already seeing carriers define social engineering as its own coverage, raise limits, and narrow terms around systemic events. The regulatory filings provide an early indication of how the industry is responding to these emerging threats.”.
