Nothing’s CMF Speaker M review: A $109 Bluetooth speaker that reveals up Bose and Sony

Recent findings suggest that nothing’s budget sub-brand, CMF, just keeps getting better and better, proving that budget tech doesn’t have to be visually unappealing, featureless, or uncool. At $109 a pop (or $79 when on sale), the CMF Speaker M is feature-forward, easily competitive with speakers from more expensive brands, and oddly satisfying to look at.

Also: 3 ways I put my Bluetooth speaker to work during a power outage.

I kept two Speaker Ms on my desk for two weeks, and I’m impressed with their performance, even though the modularity aspect is half-baked. Still, if you like speakers that resemble old-timey milk cartons or look like something out of a 2D cartoon, you’ll like the Speaker M.

According to recent reports, have you ever seen gadgets in a cartoon and wished you could use them in real life? That’s how the Speaker M feels to me. Its design is quirky, with a silhouette that mimics a milk carton, vibrant primary colors, circular cutouts for the speaker housings, and an interactive multifunction button reminiscent of a game controller.

The design is cute, fun, and interesting, and it’s a fresh take on Bluetooth speakers, which all tend to look like iterative versions of each other. The Speaker M (and the smaller Speaker S) also features modularity with removable faceplates for customization, though CMF doesn’t yet sell ready-made replacements. For the especially individualistic, you can 3D-print custom faceplates.

I’m not a fan of modularity solely for aesthetic purposes; I stand by my conviction that modular audio products should also offer additional functionality when parts are swapped. I’ve seen it plenty of times — Dyson’s OnTrac headphones have swappable earcups; Fender’s Mix has the same, and now CMF does, too, though it’s an interesting concept to introduce to Bluetooth speakers.

My point is that changing the device’s look alone isn’t a compelling enough reason to buy additional parts.

Beats’ 360 headphones are the best implementation of modularity, with swappable ear pads that not only promote individuality and customization but also serve their own purposes. Changing the earcups changes the headphones’ look and purpose.

Still, the Speaker M has a solid skill set, with Auracast, lossless audio via USB-C, NFC tap-to-pair, and enough physical controls to render your phone unnecessary when the speaker is powered on.

In the Nothing X app, you can play around with the speaker’s soundstage, equalizer, and connectivity. Stereo Mode lets you connect to two speakers and listen in a left/right channel configuration; Night Mode aims to preserve fidelity at low volume; and Low Lag Mode is self-explanatory.

In a recent development, auracast was very simple and reliable. The Speaker M has an Auracast button on the back; start a stream, press the button, and that’s that.

In a recent development, I automatically assign major brownie points to any Auracast-enabled device that bypasses the need to join a broadcast through the device’s native Bluetooth settings. Apple isn’t too keen on Auracast (yet … a girl can dream), so whenever the feature resides in the audio device’s settings, I’m already a fan.

Also: I let ChatGPT run my 7 Sonos speakers – and multi-step commands were the best part.

On to sound. The Speaker M features a 70mm woofer and a 16mm tweeter. Clarity isn’t an issue whatsoever; vocals are clear, and mid-to-high instrumentals are front and center. From Playboi Carti’s “Ahh Yeah” and Trina’s “Told Ya’ll” to Phil Collins’ “Take Me Home” and George Michael’s “Fastlove, Pt. 1,” it’s easy to hear most parts of a track clearly.

However, the Speaker M’s bass performance leaves much to be desired, and even with the volume maxed out, there’s a noticeable absence of deep-reaching frequencies. This issue becomes especially apparent when listening to an Auracast stream, as any changes I made to the equalizer were reverted.

Speaking of the Auracast pairing, putting two Speaker Ms on my desk created the ultimate workday listening experience. Though the speakers weren’t paired in a left/right channel configuration, placing one speaker to my left and the other to my right created a center-channel illusion.

I love a deep, punchy bass response, like the one I get from the Bose SoundLink Plus, but the SoundLink Plus’ four passive radiators shake and rattle my desk trinkets.

The Speaker M promises 18 hours of playtime, but I would expect a few more hours on top of that. There’s also no 3.5mm jack, but CMF hopes you’ll just use the USB-C port. If you want to take the Speaker M on outdoor adventures, it has an IP68 durability rating, meaning it’s dustproof and waterproof.

The CMF Speaker M is full of tricks, from useful audio modes and easily translated physical controls to a playful, fresh design. The Speaker M sounds great, and with some tweaking to the bass, you can get a little more of the depth that it lacks out of the box. It won’t match up to what you can get from Bose, Sony, or JBL, but we are talking about a budget brand.

The Speaker M has more features than Bose’s cheapest speaker, the $129 SoundLink Micro, and a more inspiring design than JBL’s $100 Grip and Sony’s $140 Ult Field 1. If versatility and individuality are top of mind, go with CMF.

Industry observers note that the Speaker M proves that budget products don’t have to be boring, flat, or devoid of up-to-date technologies. With Auracast on deck, the Speaker M automatically opens itself up to connecting with an unlimited number of other Auracast-enabled speakers; its design is interesting, and its USB-C audio makes it useful for creators and enjoyers alike.

There aren’t any ecosystem barriers keeping the Speaker M from being a full-featured speaker for anyone, regardless of their choice of device manufacturer, which is always a plus in my book. On top of that, most other brands would charge a premium of around $150 or more for all of the features the Speaker M offers.
