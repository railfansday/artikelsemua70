Apple iMac M6 review: Your best Apple AIO option – just go for the upgrades

Recent findings suggest that the iMac is arguably Apple’s safest product. It serves a broad purpose: serving as an at-home or office computer, and looking pretty in the process. It’s colorful and capable, and it does just about anything one would want it to for the price. It’s also swiftly become the “default” computer in commercial spaces like hotels and receptionist desks for its all-in-one, everything-included form factor.

Also: Apple’s new touchscreen MacBook Pros may be late, but they have one substantial advantage.

That’s why the 2026 model has remained mostly unchanged, save for a performance bump to match the computing demands of modern-day. If you put the latest iMac beside the one from 2024, you’d be hard-pressed to guess which is which.

In a recent development, I’m not against that approach; the newly introduced iMac looks as photogenic as ever, giving even the mundane meeting room I tested it in the life it desperately needed. But if you’re planning to upgrade your PC this year, make sure you’re investing in the right configuration. In some cases, your best option from Apple may not even be the iMac.

I’ve been testing the M6 iMac over the past week, replacing my MacBook Pro while at the office, and there are really no surprises here. The industrial design is just as uniform and stylish as I remember, with a 24-inch screen that’s encased in colorful aluminum.

It has been revealed that like its predecessor, you have the options of Blue, Purple, Pink, Orange, Yellow, Green, and Silver. My review unit came in Green, which had a balance of pastel hues and white borders. The theming extends to the included Magic Mouse, Keyboard, and cable ports for a unified look, which is a nice touch.

If you’re a major Apple fan, you can even color-match the iMac with a HomePod Mini 2 and leverage the speaker for better audio – though I’ve always found the iMac sound system good enough.

It has been revealed that the nice colors and sound won’t make me forgive Apple for the lack of options elsewhere, though. Years later, the iMac still lacks a height-adjustable and rotating stand, meaning you’ll have to settle for a single viewing orientation. (That is, unless you opt for the VESA-mounted option and set the iMac on your own adjustable stand.).

Sources indicate that being the “general purpose” iMac also means it’s limited to a 60Hz refresh rate and a 24-inch display. By now, I know many people -– family and friends, included –- who have moved on to larger screens, whether for multitasking or entertainment, so even a 27-inch option would’ve been welcome this time around.

Overall, the iMac’s design is minimal, and that’s the point; a single power cable extends from the back of the monitor, with multiple Thunderbolt ports beside it. With the base $1,499 model, you’ll get just two Thunderbolt (USB-C) ports and a headphone jack.

That may seem like enough, but I’d actually recommend most people spend the $200 more for the four-port configuration, as it doubles the internal storage (from 256GB to 512GB), unlocks all the CPU and GPU cores (from 10 cores to 12) of the M6 chipset, and supports up to 32GB of RAM. The bump up in storage alone would make the iMac investment more worthwhile.

Industry observers note that going from the M4 iMac to an M6 iMac is not as generational a jump as the M3-to-M4 transition, but the improvements still add up to a better computing and gaming experience. We’re talking more CPU and GPU cores, a new dual 16-core Neural Engine (NPU), and faster memory bandwidth. (For future-proofing, there’s also Wi-Fi 7 and Bluetooth 6 on board now.).

Recent findings suggest that put simply, the M6 iMac has become more power-efficient and can generate and render local AI workloads much more swiftly. I tested this by running a series of strictly on-device tasks, including AI denoising a batch of 50 high-ISO RAW photos in Adobe Lightroom and editing 10 horizontal 4K clips with Smart Conform in Final Cut Pro.

I ran a warm-up run on both Macs to ensure their Core ML models were optimized for the tasks, then averaged the completion times of the next two trials.

In a recent development, in both cases, the M6 iMac was faster than my M4 MacBook Pro (released in late 2024) by about half the time. Presumably, this gap would be much larger if you were upgrading from a model older than the M4, such as an M1- or Intel-based Mac.

One thing I was particularly impressed with here was the thermal management, and how the M6 iMac throttled itself later and less often than the M4 MacBook Pro, whether I was benchmarking or playing games on Apple Arcade.

I mentioned earlier that the best Mac for you, even if you previously owned an iMac, may not be the latest model. That’s because the Mac Mini is more popular than ever, and its modular nature makes it potentially the better computing choice for your setup.

The latest Mac Mini starts at $899, has the same M6 chipset, more ports (including HDMI), and can be paired with your choice of monitor and peripherals – potentially saving you costs in the end if you already have them lying around. If you’re looking for a computer to use in the home office or at work, the flexibility of that setup may be more enticing.

Recent findings suggest that however, if the uniformity and all-in-one nature of the iMac suits you better, there’s really no better or more sensible option that Apple offers right now. If you’re a first-time Mac buyer who’s upgrading from an outdated desktop (and don’t need pro-level power), the iMac is made for you. But even then, from my testing, there’s plenty of power this year to take advantage of, especially if you’re coming from an older model.
