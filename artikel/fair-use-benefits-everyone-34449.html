Authors, journalists, creators, academics, accessibility advocates, legal experts, librarians, economists and business leaders are voicing their support for the longstanding principle of fair use and its application to AI training.

For decades, fair use has played a critical role in driving innovation that benefits everyone. It has helped deliver vital medical and scientific breakthroughs and new technologies that have made real and positive differences for countless people, including authors and journalists. The New York Times’ lawsuit, meanwhile, asks everyone to ignore a half century of legal history and progress, and toss aside the longstanding principle of fair use in their own narrow pursuit of an undeserved payday.

Experts and leaders across a wide range of fields have recognized that fair use is a win-win that benefits everyone, and have shared their perspectives with the Court in filing briefs in support of fair use.

Sources indicate that below you’ll find excerpts from some of those submissions, in their own words. The full filings are available on the public court docket, and you can read more about fair use and AI here.

Sources indicate that this case concerns more than companies that develop large language models (LLMs). It affects medical researchers and patients, educators serving students with different learning needs, people with disabilities seeking greater independence, farmers applying agricultural knowledge, independent creators developing fresh forms of expression, and industry sectors from software to the law. These communities share an interest in the ability to study and learn from broad and diverse sources of knowledge, and in access to the tools that make that learning useful. Many depend on systems developed by others, including open source AI systems, rather than training models themselves. Fair use supports their ability to learn, discover, communicate, and create. Restrictions on fair use training would affect both the institutions building AI and the people using it.

Industry observers note that to the extent AI training enables the creation of newly introduced knowledge, expression, software, and other capabilities, those benefits bear on factor four’s assessment of the use’s market effects. That reasoning supports a novel application here: considering the national-security value of capabilities that enable new analysis, software, and other productive work.

Professor of Strategic Management.

On the record in this case, the defendants’ use of plaintiffs’ works for AI training is highly transformative and strongly favors a finding of fair use because under realistic conditions the outputs of defendants’ models are in no relevant way similar to the invisible intermediate copies made during the training process.

Musician and composer / comedian and writer.

According to recent reports, generalization and memorization are distinct phenomena. Training on a large and varied collection enables a model to respond to prompts not encountered during training, supporting the conclusion that it learns patterns applicable to fresh contexts rather than simply storing source works. A trained model is not an addressable document repository and does not ordinarily retrieve identified training data as stored files. There is no address, index, or file handle by which particular training data can be requested and returned; generation instead proceeds by producing a probability distribution for the next token and selecting from it.

It has been revealed that fair use has protected tools that help people navigate the vast collections of information the digital world has brought us, including image search and full-text book search. See Perfect 10, Inc. v. Amazon.com, Inc., 508 F.3d 1146, 1164–68 (9th Cir. 2007); Authors Guild v. Google, Inc., 804 F.3d 202, 216–17, 225 (2d Cir. 2015) (“Google Books”). It has also protected libraries’ provision of accessible digital books to readers with print disabilities. See Authors Guild, Inc. v. HathiTrust, 755 F.3d 87, 101–03 (2d Cir. 2014). And it has protected free online publication of technical standards incorporated into law, recognizing the “substantial public benefits of free and easy access to the law.” Am. Soc’y for Testing & Materials v. Public.Resource.Org, Inc., 82 F.4th 1262, 1271–72 (D.C. Cir. 2023). Fair use will be equally beneficial in ensuring that AI’s benefits accrue to the public at large, instead of being controlled by a privileged few.

Pulitzer Prize-winning journalist / Professor of Law.

OpenAI’s use of newspaper articles to train its generative AI models didn’t harm the reasonable commercial expectations of the News Plaintiffs in any way. They commissioned and published newspaper articles with the expectation of selling consumers access to those articles. They retain and continue to exercise the exclusive right to sell consumers access to their articles. Their ability to sell consumers access to their articles wasn’t affected by OpenAI’s use of those articles to train its AI models.
