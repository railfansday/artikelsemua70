Tidal announces ban on AI-generated music; 27 million tracks will be deleted

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Most streaming music providers have opted to label AI-generated content on their platforms, and until now, Tidal was one of them. However, the tech giant is about to take a harder stance. Tidal declared that in the coming weeks, it will fully ban AI music because of the harm it does to human artists.

Sources indicate that tidal debuted its initial AI policy in July 2026, which included labeling and demonetizing all songs created entirely with AI. That wasn’t enough to stem the tide of AI slop, though. Over the past 90 days, Tidal reports that 43 percent of latest uploads were completely AI-generated. On November 6, new uploads to Tidal hit a whopping 60 percent AI-generated.

Sources indicate that as things stand, Tidal hosts 27 million tracks labeled as AI, all of which were obviously added in the past couple of years. That accounts for 6.3 percent of Tidal’s total catalog. On December 9, Tidal will delete all those songs. The streamer notes this will have essentially no negative impact, because 96 percent of AI-labeled tracks got zero plays in the past 90 days. Throughout its announcement, Tidal is careful to stipulate that it’s only talking about music that has been fully created by AI. Artists can still use AI tools as long as the final product contains human elements.

Credit: Tidal Credit: Tidal.

Some people don’t particularly mind AI music, but the team at Tidal has come to believe the harm to artists is too great. AI models can pump out random noises faster than humans can create things of real value, which can mislead listeners and siphon royalty payments away from creators. There’s also an matter with AI uploaders who are actively attempting to steal royalty payments. Tidal indicates it has already removed about 66,000 tracks from its service for impersonating artists, which was against its AI policy even before this revision.

As of December 9, individual uploaders and distributors will no longer be allowed to add AI music to Tidal. However, they could lie, couldn’t they?

According to recent reports, tidal hasn’t specified how it detects AI music, but it will probably rely on watermarking. Almost every AI song on Tidal (94 percent) comes from Suno, which added watermarking earlier this year. Tidal also cites Google Lyria as a source of AI tunes, which has SynthID labels embedded in the waveform. Deezer claimed it has a licensable AI music detector with a false-positive rate of less than 0.01 percent, but it’s unclear if Tidal is using that or something similar.

Industry observers note that that will take care of most AI music, but this approach won’t detect everything. There are open models for music generation that you can run locally on a GPU, and some cloud-based providers aren’t bothering to watermark their outputs. Even if Tidal is using a separate detection system, new models may be able to evade it—it’s always going to be a cat-and-mouse game. This will be a problem with verifying provenance in all types of media in the future. For the time being, at least, Tidal’s policy should clear out most of the AI slop.

The people directing robots to make these songs will have plenty of other options after Tidal blocks AI. Spotify, YouTube Music, Deezer, and Apple Music all allow AI music on their platforms with labeling. Spotify has removed millions of AI tracks from its collection based on user feedback. Other streamers haven’t specified how much slop (if any) they’ve cleared out.
