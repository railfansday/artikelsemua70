While putting together the off-grid solar power and diesel heating is well within my skill set, woodworking isn’t a strong point for me. I can do it, but there’s always so much time between projects that skill fade has kicked in, and I always feel like I’m reinventing the wheel.

According to recent reports, one thing that makes a tough job easier is having the right tools, and one tool in particular that I appreciate more than most nowadays is a good electric screwdriver. I went for years putting screwdriver bits onto whatever drill I owned at the time, but once I used my first dedicated electric screwdriver, I saw the light. I’ve been using the HOTO PixelDrive for about a year now, and that’s been great — but I’m always looking for a new tool!

For this cabin build I’ve been putting the Fanttik S2 Pro through its paces, and no matter how hard I’ve pushed it, it’s kept delivering.

And this screwdriver has a trick up its sleeve that separates it from the rest.

The S2 Pro is a chunky, palm-sized unit that holds a 2,000mAh rechargeable battery.

According to recent reports, it has a 250-RPM motor with seven torque settings, from a soft 0.5Nm to a beefy 6Nm (4.5 to 50lb-in). You alter the torque output by rotating the collar at the top of the screwdriver, and your current setting is shown on a colorful bar indicator.

A single charge is good enough to drive some 1,500 M3 x 10mm screws (longer or thicker screws like the ones I’ve been driving will reduce this… and I’ve been driving some monster screws), and when it’s recharge time, it’s a matter of hooking it up to a convenient USB-C charger.

At the business end of the screwdriver is a shadowless LED for shining a light at what you’re doing, which means an end to holding a flashlight in your mouth as you crawl about underneath something trying to get some fasteners in place.

Recent findings suggest that fanttik says that the S2 Pro is best suited to “effortlessly handling common household tasks such as computer assembly, furniture installation, and home appliance repairs.” A 6Nm electric screwdriver is a light-duty tool. Sure, it’s stronger than most people’s wrists, especially after the first dozen or so screws. Most hands can manage around 2 to 5Nm, though someone with gorilla hands might reach 10Nm.

Also, it doesn’t get tired — unless you think of recharging as a nap.

It has been revealed that and when you’re putting together flat-pack furniture or a PC, too much torque — especially in the hands of someone who is inexperienced — can be destructive. Electrical fittings like outlets are especially vulnerable, and too much torque can crack an expensive faceplate.

If the S2 Pro can help put a cabin together, it can handle most of the day-to-day tasks you have around the home. Sure, if you’re putting decking down or a fence up, or driving coach screws or lag bolts, then a more powerful drill/driver or impact driver will make the job quicker, but that’s a different animal.

According to recent reports, the S2 Pro comes with 20 different 1/4-inch bits covering all the popular sizes of slotted, Phillips, Pozidriv, hex, and Torx fasteners that you’re going to come across, plus a 2.3-inch extension rod for when you need a bit of extra reach. Made of S2 steel, these bits are more than enough for most people, and if you’re like me, you’re more likely to lose them than break them.

Nothing new there, but what is special to the S2 Pro is that you get a right-angle adapter for screwing around corners.

Just what you need for getting at the screw holding a drawer knob in place, or up into some awkward spot when assembling a wardrobe or bed. You don’t realize how handy one of these is until you have one and remember all the times you struggled to get an electric screwdriver into a difficult location, or you gave up and went old-school with a stubby screwdriver.

I especially like that the angled head attaches magnetically and can sit in one of four orientations, so you’re never struggling to use the controls on the screwdriver’s body. It’s a nice touch that demonstrates that the people who made this actually use tools.

There are a couple of limitations that come with the right-angle adapter.

First, you lose some torque. I’ve not got hard numbers here, but I’d say it feels like about 5 to 20%. This is mostly down to friction in the adapter’s gearing, and it’s more noticeable at lower torque settings.

A bigger issue than the torque loss is that the right-angle adapter means you can’t put as much weight behind the screw, and there’s a tendency for the bit to cam out and slip, especially with Phillips and slotted-head fasteners. Pozidriv fasteners are better at resisting cam-out and slipping, with hex and Torx better still. Most of the screws I’ve been driving in with this screwdriver have been Torx, and I’ve really grown to appreciate how good this fastener design actually is.

When using the right-angle adapter, go for hex or Torx if you can because they work so much better, and if you have to use a different, lesser fastener, take your time and work more slowly.

I’ve been impressed by the Fanttik S2 Pro. It’s a great tool that’s handled everything I’ve thrown at it, including jobs way beyond what the manufacturer notes it’s built to do.

It feels comfortable in the hand, even for long jobs that involve driving hundreds of floorboard screws, and it’s not so aggressive that it causes blisters (if you want more grip, a pair of gloves makes a major difference).

Recent findings suggest that I also like the torque indicator’s bar chart. It’s clear, easy to read, and goes a long way toward stopping you from cranking down hard on something breakable.

Sources indicate that the inclusion of a right-angle adapter is also something I appreciate, especially one custom-fit to the screwdriver rather than a generic one. I’ve messed around with the flexible and pivoting extensions in the past, and they’re nowhere near as good as this fixed 90-degree adapter.

With a sticker price of $99, the Fanttik S2 Pro is a good buy, but it usually sells over on Amazon for about $70, which makes it a really good deal.
