CNET has a piece that concurs with my observations, calling Muse “a potential security nightmare.” It documented a number of cases where, despite permissions being turned off, researchers claim Muse harvested private data. I haven’t seen this behavior in my testing, but it is a substantial part of why I’m very careful with how I use it.

So while I use Muse regularly, I never give it any personal details. I don’t let it have any access to my computer or my computer’s microphone. I never give it an assignment that requires computer use or access to any of my accounts. I don’t set up any connectors, including to my Mac apps like Notepad or Reminders.

And I most definitely do not give it access to Chrome, let it record my screen, or give it access to my payment methods through my Stripe account or Shop Pay.

In a recent development, in addition to these obvious “what not to do” items, I also don’t use Muse interactively as a chatbot. I don’t converse with Muse. During chats, you are likely to reveal snippets of personal information. We do not want Meta to have any of that personal information. For any of the standard chatbot-type activities, I use a more trustworthy tool.

In a recent development, put simply, Muse’s free agentic capabilities are pretty good. As an AI researcher, I need to use it just because it’s out there and it’s popular. I test these things as part of my job. But I would use it anyway because it has a fairly unique value proposition.

As a basic AI tool, it’s as good as anything we had in 2025. In terms of late 2026 technology, I’d put it on a par with ChatGPT Terra or Luna, or Claude Sonnet or Haiku. Muse is definitely not at the flagship model level like ChatGPT Sol or Claude Opus, but it’s not bad.

Recent findings suggest that muse’s value prop has to do with how much agentic work it’s willing to do for free. All the frontier model providers meter their agentic capabilities in one way or another. For applications using the models, the AI companies sell API access by tokens. For desktop work, you can buy subscriptions, typically at $20, $100, and $200 per month.

For those monthly subscription fees, you get a certain amount of usage: less on the lower tiers, more if you’re a bigger spender. I have the $20-per-month ChatGPT Plus initiative, which gets me a small amount of programming and a moderate amount of agentic work a month.

I have the $100-per-month Claude Max strategy, which usually gets me through a month of fairly heavy vibe coding, but I do use up my weekly allocation from time to time.

I am constantly aware of how I am consuming my allocated usage. I don’t really want to pay more than I already am. So I try to spend my usage allocation where it will have the most impact.

Muse gives me an agentic tool with free usage allocation. I can give Muse non-mission-critical assignments and not worry about whether or not I’ll run out of usage. If I do, I do. I’m not using it as part of my day-to-day projects.

Recent findings suggest that that’s Muse’s value. You can give it work that requires agentic capabilities, but limit it to assignments that are not worth upgrading one of your other paid subscriptions for.

I use Muse exclusively for agentic search and analysis. I give it assignments that require a chain of web searches for information-gathering purposes. Those searches generate data that needs analysis and intelligence as the chain is processed, which then results in a report or recommendation. It’s really quite good at that.

Sources indicate that my interest profile is probably a matter of public record. Heck, if you’ve been reading my articles for any time at all, you know what I’m interested in. And you know that I’m a bit of a geek. So I don’t mind the risk that Facebook will pick up on this.
