Tech Report: Testing artefacts are leaking data your compliance misses

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Automated testing helps protect software quality, but according to Napier AI, the reports, screenshots, logs and file exports it produces each day may be one of the least controlled data surfaces in many organisations. As these artefacts move between teams, vendors and stakeholders, the sensitive information inside them can create serious privacy and compliance exposure.

Napier AI notes that test environments often hold real or realistic data, such as customer names, account numbers, contact details and financial figures. That data appears in the interfaces under test and is captured in the outputs. A report is attached to a ticket, a screenshot lands in a defect log, or a file export is sent to a vendor for troubleshooting. Each action seems routine, yet each one carries sensitive data further from the systems built to protect it.

It has been revealed that the RegTech specialist argues that traditional redaction methods have not kept up. A decade ago, test suites ran on fixed schedules and produced a manageable number of outputs that a person could review before sharing. Today, continuous integration pipelines can trigger thousands of test runs a day. AI-assisted development is adding to this, as AI tools generate more code and test cases, often faster than governance frameworks can adapt.

Most existing redaction, Napier AI indicates, sits outside the workflow as an add-on. It might be a manual review, a plugin someone has to remember to switch on, or a post-processing step that gets dropped when deadlines tighten. These approaches also tend to focus on structured data fields, which leaves screenshots of live interfaces largely unprotected.

It has been revealed that the financial risks are significant. GDPR penalties can reach €20m or 4% of global annual turnover, whichever is greater, and IBM’s Cost of a Data Breach Report 2026 puts the global average breach cost at $4.99m. Napier AI adds that the damage to customer trust may be even more costly.

Sources indicate that for Napier AI, the core problem is structural: protection that relies on human vigilance is fragile by design. Its answer is compliance by design, built on three principles. First, redaction should happen inside the pipeline, at the moment an artefact is created. Second, coverage must include screenshots and file exports as well as reports. Third, every tool should pass security and compliance approval and hold recognised certifications such as SOC 2. Even then, sensitive data should be redacted before it reaches the tool.

Recent findings suggest that the firm claims this approach also improves productivity. Teams can share outputs without manual checks, audits move faster, and organisations can share artefacts with partners, auditors or regulators with more confidence.

Napier AI has applied these principles to its own practices. It developed its auto-redaction capability in house and built it into its automation testing and reporting across reports, screenshots and files. Every tool in that workflow passed security review before being allowed into its environment.

The company compares bolt-on compliance to a seatbelt that someone has to remember to fasten. Built-in protection, by contrast, works more like a car’s crumple zones, which function whether anyone thinks about them or not. Its central question for organisations is whether sensitive data is protected at the point of creation or only after it has already been exposed.
