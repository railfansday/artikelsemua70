Want to try ChatGPT Wallet? Read OpenAI’s liability disclaimer very carefully first

🌐 Source: Zdnet • 📅 17 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 11 min read (2174 words, 📚 Deep Dive)
💡 Key Takeaways

  • Not to be outdone in its quest to gobble up consumer engagement, OpenAI introduced Wallet in ChatGPT last week, which lets ChatGPT spend your money for you.
  • Also: I connected ChatGPT to my bank, and it’s my go-to finance app now.
📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

In a recent development, the AI companies want your money. That’s been obvious, but now they want to manage your money. In May, OpenAI introduced Finances for ChatGPT for Plus tiers and above. Then in October, OpenAI opened the service to free and Go tier owners as well. Then, in early November, Anthropic countered with Personal Finance in Claude.

Not to be outdone in its quest to gobble up consumer engagement, OpenAI introduced Wallet in ChatGPT last week, which lets ChatGPT spend your money for you.

Also: I connected ChatGPT to my bank, and it’s my go-to finance app now.

It has been revealed that today, Wallet requires your approval. But OpenAI’s blog post makes it clear that the company eventually wants its AI agents to make purchases entirely autonomously. I’m not signing up. OpenAI disclaims liability. There is a huge potential for AI mistakes. There are the usual AI privacy concerns, as well as worrisome security risks. Let’s dig in.

ChatGPT Wallet is another in a series of tools designed to give AI agents access to secured information without actually sharing information with the agents. I discussed this approach earlier in the year with 1Password’s integration with Claude, their Unified Access credential management tool, and Microsoft’s Agent 365 identity management tools.

The core difference is that ChatGPT Wallet is focused specifically on facilitating credit card purchases, not logins in general. OpenAI worked with American Express, Mastercard, and Visa to develop the tool, which it notes, “lets agents pay without access to users’ actual card details and that requires a security check for every purchase, while also preserving existing card rewards and protections for eligible disputes.”.

Sources indicate that openAI indicates that purchases must be reviewed by people before they’re made, and all purchases must go through a security check, such as Face ID or a fingerprint scan. Then the payment network passes a payment token (a code, not a unit of AI work) rather than actual credit card information. Payments are charged without ChatGPT Wallet ever sharing real credit card details.

Also: I tested OpenAI Dot and Anthropic Duet for weeks – all I got was one helpful notification.

According to recent reports, right now, Wallet is limited to Pro-tier OpenAI users. It’s also limited to use inside of Dot.

So far, this approach doesn’t seem all that different from Apple Pay or Google Pay online. But then there’s this. OpenAI says, “Over time, we expect users to be able to delegate purchases entirely to their agent.” OpenAI says that you’ll be able to set payment parameters, limits, categories, and time periods, but still. If you use this service, you’ll be letting the AI buy stuff for you.

But there are differences between that and what ChatGPT Wallet will do, and here’s where I’ll enumerate my reasons why I don’t want an AI spending my money for me.

In a recent development, as such things go, OpenAI’s 1,597 word ChatGPT Wallet terms document isn’t obnoxiously long or difficult to understand. I read the whole thing, and here’s the all-caps block of text that caught my attention:.

OPENAI DOES NOT ASSUME ANY LIABILITY FOR PURCHASES, PAYMENTS, FUNDS TRANSFERS, ORDERS, ORDER FULFILMENT, RECEIPT INFORMATION, ACCESS, IDENTITY VERIFICATION, TRANSACTIONS, THIRD PARTY USE OR TREATMENT OF USER DATA, OR OTHER ACTIVITY MADE USING THE CHATGPT WALLET, AND YOU AGREE TO LOOK SOLELY TO AGREEMENTS YOU MAY HAVE WITH YOUR CARD ISSUER, PAYMENT NETWORK, FINANCIAL INSTITUTION, MERCHANT, APP DEVELOPER, STATE ISSUING AUTHORITY, OR OTHER APPLICABLE THIRD PARTY TO RESOLVE ANY QUESTIONS OR DISPUTES RELATING TO YOUR SUPPORTED CARDS AND ASSOCIATED ACTIVITIES.

Industry observers note that the first four words are the key: OpenAI does not assume any liability. So, if you use its AI, and its AI runs amok (as AIs are wont to do), and said AI winds up spending your actual money, OpenAI’s best answer is that you should take up your dispute with the bank.

It has been revealed that what could possibly go wrong? The agent could purchase the wrong item or hundreds of them. It could misinterpret your priorities. It could use the wrong delivery address. It could misunderstand a merchant’s return policy. Need I go on? And if something goes wrong, OpenAI explicitly and officially will not have your back.

Recent findings suggest that last week, I indicated you how I used Claude and Claude Sheets to help me pick out a fresh water flosser. My Waterpik died and I wanted a new one right away.

In a recent development, the AI did a great job, finding some available same-day from Walmart and the next day from Amazon Prime. The thing was, the Walmart ones had a water reservoir that was too small, but they met my requirement for “want it now.” The Amazon ones met my requirement for reservoir size, but wouldn’t get to me until the next day.

Also: I tried Claude Sheets on 3 tasks – and it saved me hours of work in 10 minutes.

As it turned out, the bigger reservoir was more crucial to me, so I waited the day. But if I had left this purchase entirely in the hands of an AI, it might have ordered the too-small Walmart offering.

To be fair, this kind of “choose something for me” delegation could go wrong if you ask an assistant or even a spouse to buy something for you. But sellers are much more used to humans making purchases than they are dealing with AI-generated purchases.

According to recent reports, also, fortunately, most places I purchase from have generous return policies. But what if the AI didn’t buy from somewhere I have an established relationship with?

Sources indicate that also: Are vertical mice a gimmick? I tested 9 models with my wrist pain.

According to recent reports, last month, I asked Gemini Spark (Google’s agentic AI) to find a vertical mouse for me on Amazon. I knew Amazon sold these, because I did an entire article comparing nine of them. But Gemini Spark presented purchase options from OzBargain, Pacifiko, and Ugreen, companies I had never dealt with before.

According to recent reports, it appears Amazon blocks some of Google’s AI agents from accessing its site, which could explain why Spark didn’t return any Amazon listings. Still, what if it had decided to buy something from one of those other retailers without my permission? OzBargain is on the other side of the planet in Australia, and Pacifiko is in Guatemala, halfway down Central America. There could have been currency issues, enormous shipping costs, unknown return challenges, and more.You’d think Google’s AI would check Amazon when I specifically asked it to, or at least explain why it couldn’t. But it did neither. So what makes you think an AI with access to your credit card will always follow your instructions?

Isn’t it bad enough that OpenAI can profile you completely from your ChatGPT conversations? Or that it and the myriad of other AIs were trained on all of the hard work we’ve done by writing and publishing on the Internet. Heck, I know for a fact that Claude hoovered in at least one book I wrote back in the mid-1990s.

Industry observers note that but still, why give OpenAI more? I spent a little time reading OpenAI’s Wallet Privacy Notice and noticed some privacy concerns. The document says OpenAI can collect:.

It has been revealed that the manufacturer indicates it may share relevant information with payment processors, merchants, issuers, and security providers. To a degree, this is necessary in order to facilitate transactions. OpenAI, to its credit, says it won’t sell Wallet information or use it to sell behavioral advertising data.

Sources indicate that but still, here’s another place where your personal financial information (and purchasing preferences) will be stored. I’m not thrilled with this at all.

Prompt injection is a malicious practice where scammers hide AI instructions inside regular web pages, and spoof the AIs into interpreting those hidden instructions as real prompts.

The AI companies are actively hardening their products against prompt injection, but an OpenAI document from last year indicates they’re still struggling with it.

It has been revealed that more to the point, the OpenAI page on Dot security won’t commit to fully protecting against prompt injection. It says, “These protections help reduce the risk of malicious instructions causing an unwanted action, but they do not eliminate it.”.

An attacker certainly could attempt to poison e-commerce pages, especially as more and more AIs can virtually swipe the plastic. E-commerce pages are also, by their very nature, designed to sell. So a credit card-enabled AI might already be trying to make sense of misleading or overly promotional information and might get confused.

That’s a real risk and when you combine two statements from OpenAI, the “they do not eliminate it” speaking of risk in the Dot security page and the “OpenAI does not assume any liability” from the Wallet terms document, you get what’s essentially a statement saying, “Well, it’s risky, but don’t come complaining to us if something goes wrong.”.

Recent findings suggest that that has the same feeling as those disclaimer forms you have to sign before skydiving or bungee jumping. You can do it, but don’t blame the operator if it all goes to crap.

Some shopping carts will block AI agents. Amazon already blocks anything from Meta’s Muse. Smaller vendors might not know what to do about the interop between AI agents and their cart software.

OpenAI indirectly acknowledges this in its blog post, saying, “Alongside ChatGPT Wallet, we’re releasing tools and guidance to help businesses support customers using agents.”.

Also: Meta Muse is the worst AI agent for privacy – and I’ve tried them all.

Sources indicate that it’s possible shopping carts could stall, customers could wind up with the wrong item (or a hundred of the wrong item), or any other mishap you could imagine. Running an online shopping cart is a constant challenge. My software cart system has been acting up all week, and both the cart vendor and I have had at least 10 back-and-forth interactions trying to understand what’s happening.

Add to that the possibility that an AI is making a purchase, or can’t figure out the cart, or has decided to pound on the cart 100,000 times in order to get it to finally do what it wants, and you’re in fresh hell territory.

According to recent reports, then, what if it buys the wrong stuff? Going back to the seller and explaining, “Well, my AI did it,” is not going to go over well while you’re asking for a refund.

Industry observers note that my wife has a theory. She claims that some stores will flat-out refuse to accept AI purchases. She also predicts that some stores will put AI disclaimers in their online fine print saying no purchases made by AI will be refunded.

While I was looking into this topic, I spoke with some experts for their input.

In a recent development, harold Byun is CEO of BlueRock, a security platform for AI agents. In an email to me, he pointed out that “Early internet e-commerce faced similar resistance.” He mentioned that, “We can argue that this time, with AI, it’s different and there’s a lot of evidence to support that. In all of those prior scenarios, the security got better, and it helped give people confidence to use those technologies.”.

He indicates that with over 60% of information searches going through AI, it becomes an inevitability that the next step for people is to transact, or research and buy. Over time, he said, the security will get better. He acknowledged that many won’t be willing to give an agent their credit card right now. But he said, “I think there are people that will always hold that position, but there are many where speed and ease of transacting will be the deciding factor.”.

In a recent development, arijit Sengupta is founder and CEO of Aible, a manufacturer that deploys governed, cost-efficient enterprise AI. He’s also the former CEO of a Salesforce subsidiary. He makes two very interesting points.

It has been revealed that in an email, he told me, “One of the things that has been proven already is that with autonomous agents, people are kind of careful the first few times they are asked to approve something, and then they start just approving all, and that is likely to happen in this situation too. Once you start trusting the agent and start approving everything without thought, that’s when you actually get hit with a really bad outcome.”.

His second point goes directly to my concern over OpenAI’s disclaiming of responsibility. He wrote, “Both OpenAI and Anthropic have had data breaches of various sorts. Can they really guarantee, given their increasing use of autonomous agents internally, that they can protect their customer data?”.
