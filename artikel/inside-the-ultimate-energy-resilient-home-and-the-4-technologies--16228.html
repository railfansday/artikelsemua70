My own home, while newly equipped with rooftop solar panels, doesn’t yet boast the full stack of tech (more on this later) needed for the scenario above, so I found one that does and booked a trip to gather some hands-on, experiential data.

Back in the smart cottage, my workday started with a glance at the energy management app and the realization that the battery had put in a full shift before the sun came up.

The home had run almost entirely on battery power through the night, using about a sixth of its capacity by the time I started the workday.

This is not a large home with heavy electrical loads. With no air conditioning and gas heat, the overnight draw was little more than the refrigerator, a few lights, and the system itself. Nevertheless, from sundown the evening before until the morning, the house drew next to nothing from the power grid and didn’t make much of a dent in the battery either.

That is, until I plugged in the EV….

Sources indicate that watching in real time on the app as I plugged in the level 2 EV charger, I saw the home’s power consumption spike drastically, with the car alone pulling 11.7 kilowatts. To put things in perspective, that’s roughly the power draw of about 10 average American homes combined at any given moment.

According to recent reports, in about 30-40 minutes, the battery dropped from 50% to its preset reserve of 25%. At that point, the battery stopped discharging, as programmed, and the grid picked up the full load of the home and the car’s remaining charge.

Tolerance for power outages varies widely across households. While some have little to no glitch with a full day or more without power, others are thrown for a loop with just a few minutes of downtime. People who work from home tend to fall closer to the latter camp. If you lose power for two hours, you could lose far more due to diminished productivity and catch-up time.

For this outage simulation, I wanted to see how gracefully the system handled some larger loads, but mainly I wanted to test Wi-Fi and workday continuity.

Sources indicate that with some assistance, we fired up several appliances, including the main furnace, dishwasher, and coffee maker, and I set up a live conference call on my laptop. Still largely drained from the EV charging earlier, the battery was at about 25%.

Industry observers note that when the main power breaker was flipped, there was a brief flicker (less than 1 second) during which the lights dimmed and then came back on. The Wi-Fi didn’t seem to skip a beat, and the conference call remained up and running with no noticeable blips or freeze frames.

A few questions were circling in my head, so I looked at the app, which answered all of them and a few that I didn’t think to ask:.

“A grid outage started 1:43 p.m.”.

“Your solar will generate 1.6 kWh more energy before 6:15 p.m.”.

“Your system should power your home until 5:00 p.m.”.

Despite a largely depleted battery, I was up and running with essentially zero disruption and a clear path to a full work day even with extra appliances running.

In a recent development, not every layer of this energy-unified home was stress-tested at its maximum. However, the app made it very easy to see how all these components work together to create automation, resilience, and cost efficiency.
