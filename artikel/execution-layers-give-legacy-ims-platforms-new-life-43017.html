Execution layers give legacy IMS platforms new life

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

For many large institutional buy-side firms, the trading desk is where the strain is showing. The core investment platform often works well. According to LSEG Data & Analytics, the answer may be to build around that platform rather than replace it.

LSEG Data & Analytics notes that the investment management system (IMS) or order management system (OMS) typically remains the backbone of the investment process. It holds positions, enforces mandate compliance, generates orders, supports allocations and feeds accounting and operations. Years of configuration and controls have been built up around it. The weakness, LSEG argues, often lies in execution, where traders need better access to venues and brokers, more automation and a clearer audit trail of how orders were worked.

A specialist order and execution management system (OEMS), or a multi-asset execution layer, can sit alongside the existing investment platform without displacing it, depending on architecture and integration needs.

Industry observers note that in this model, the investment platform creates the order, applies compliance and stays the authoritative source for positions and books and records. Once the order reaches the desk, the execution layer takes over. Traders get tools to work orders across venues, apply execution rules and manage partial fills. Portfolio managers keep working in a familiar environment.

The quality of the connection between the two layers has become more vital. As major European markets move towards T+1 settlement, LSEG Data & Analytics points out that firms will have less time to fix breaks between execution, allocation and downstream processing.

The integration has to carry far more than the initial order and the final fill. Orders must leave the IMS or OMS with the right identifiers, account details and instructions. Executions and status updates must flow back promptly. Amendments and cancellations need a clear route, even while fills are still arriving. Allocation ownership must be explicit, so the two systems do not hold competing versions of block orders that span several accounts. Both systems also need to identify instruments the same way, and a shared reference data source can reduce reconciliation work.

It has been revealed that A simple filled order is easy to handle. A partially filled order that has been amended, split across several routes and substituted into another bond is a much harder test. LSEG adds that how these events are handled also shapes the quality of data available for automation, analytics and AI.

Clear ownership is essential. If a trader sees one working quantity and a portfolio manager sees another, reconciliation becomes an operating model problem. Each system should own defined data and actions, and any disagreement between them should be visible.

Firms do not need to move the whole desk at once. LSEG Data & Analytics suggests starting where pressure is greatest. Fixed income is one example, where RFQs, list trading, broker routes and voice activity are often spread across separate workflows. Derivatives automation, API-driven equities, commodities and FX are other candidates.

According to recent reports, buyers should also test failure scenarios, not just straight-through processing. LSEG recommends asking which order states are supported, how amendments are acknowledged, how failures are recovered, and who owns an order stuck between systems.
