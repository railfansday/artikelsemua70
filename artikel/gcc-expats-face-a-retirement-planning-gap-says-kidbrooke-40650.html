GCC expats face a retirement planning gap, says Kidbrooke

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Recent findings suggest that for the millions of expatriates who build careers across the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC), retirement planning is rarely simple. In a recent report, Kidbrooke argues that banks, insurers and workplace savings providers risk leaving these customers poorly served unless they rethink how retirement services are designed.

According to recent reports, the challenge comes from the way expatriate lives actually unfold. A professional might start out in Dubai, take a later role in Saudi Arabia and finally retire in India or somewhere else entirely. Over that journey they can build up end-of-service benefits, employer-funded savings and personal investments, often spread across separate accounts and several currencies.

Sources indicate that according to Kidbrooke, knowing the total amount saved is only part of the picture. Customers also need to understand what that money could pay them each month in retirement, how long it is likely to last and whether it will meet living costs in the country where they choose to settle.

It has been revealed that kidbrooke explores these problems in its fresh white paper, Retirement Across Borders: Designing the Retirement Experience for Retail Expats in the GCC. The paper sets out how financial institutions can respond with more connected, flexible and personalised planning experiences.

Retirement savings vary across the GCC depending on the country, the employment arrangement and the individual. In the UAE, for instance, expatriate staff may receive a traditional end-of-service gratuity or join a funded workplace savings scheme. Other GCC states have their own benefit structures and regulatory rules, and many customers also hold savings and investments outside their employer’s arrangements.

Kidbrooke’s paper reviews arrangements in all six GCC countries: the UAE, Saudi Arabia, Bahrain, Qatar, Kuwait and Oman. It considers how these differences should shape customer journeys, so that people can see how each pot of money contributes to their retirement intends without losing track of when funds become available or which rules apply.

Sources indicate that A savings balance, Kidbrooke notes, claims little about the lifestyle someone can afford in later life. An expatriate saving in the UAE but planning to retire in India faces a mix of variables, including future contributions, investment returns, exchange rates, withdrawal periods and local living costs.

It has been revealed that the white paper shows how institutions can help customers bring these factors together to answer practical questions. These include what monthly income their savings might generate, how retiring abroad would change their spending power, and what happens if they contribute more, retire later or need their money to last longer. Customers will also want to know whether their income could cover essentials such as housing, healthcare and family commitments.

Using an illustrative customer scenario, Kidbrooke demonstrates how shifts in contributions, retirement duration and exchange rates can alter the income available to a household.

Kidbrooke stresses that a salaried employee in a workplace scheme has very different needs from a self-employed professional with irregular income. Those changing employers or moving between GCC countries may also need ongoing access to past benefit statements, savings records and outstanding claims.

It has been revealed that the paper maps out practical journeys covering workplace enrolment, independent saving, cross-border relocation and the move from building savings to drawing an income. It also highlights the role of accessible digital tools, clear projections and customer control in supporting better decisions.

As workplace savings arrangements evolve across the region, Kidbrooke sees a clear opportunity for providers to connect customers’ financial data with the retirement choices they face. Doing so means going beyond account balances and bringing benefit calculations, personal savings, income projections and changing circumstances together in one consistent planning experience.

It has been revealed that drawing on research, regulatory considerations and design examples, the paper outlines approaches institutions can test. It also proposes a pilot framework for measuring customer understanding, affordability, continuity and operational accuracy.
