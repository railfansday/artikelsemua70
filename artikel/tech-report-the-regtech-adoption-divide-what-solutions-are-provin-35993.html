Tech Report: The RegTech adoption divide: what solutions are proving popular?

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

RegTech has promptly grown into a booming market, with use cases across all areas of compliance. While solutions of all types are being adopted, some areas are seeing a much greater level of deployment.

Sources indicate that regTech Analyst and Parker & Lawrence Research recently released a major industry report looking at the global state of RegTech in 2026. Among its findings, the report noted that financial crime solutions have the highest rate of adoption within compliance, but why is that the case?

To get some thoughts as to why some areas are seeing greater adoption, FinTech Global recently sat down with several leading players in RegTech to get their thoughts on why financial crime solutions are seeing the greatest level of adoption in the sector.

As part of the report, which can be downloaded here, it set out to build greater structure to the fragmented market. It introduced a simplified RegTech taxonomy that covers 24 subcategories across six major risk and compliance domains. Using this framework, the report established a RegTech Adoption Index to measure relative maturity across the sector.

Recent findings suggest that at the top of that list was financial crime, boasting an Index score of 68. This domain includes solutions that are designed to detect, prevent and respond to crimes impacting financial institutions, such as fraud, money laundering, sanctions evasion and terrorist financing.

Subsectors in this domain include sanctions screening, KYC/KYB, fraud prevention/FRAML, and transaction monitoring. The leading adopters of this technology are payment firms and investment banks.

Financial crime solutions lead the way.

There are a number of reasons that could explain why financial crime solutions have seen the greatest adoption within compliance.

For Chaitanya Sarda, CEO at AiPrise, it is because it is the only domain where all three conditions for adoption are present at once.

It has been revealed that sarda remarked, “There’s a named obligation with a deadline. There’s a measurable cost of failure, in fines and enforcement actions that appear in public. And there’s a testable output, since you can check whether a screening decision was right.”.

Sources indicate that this reason also explains other domains with high adoption, such as Information and Technology Security, which has the second highest Index score of 61. This is a space where failure is immediate, visible and priced.

In a similar vein, Nir Carciente, head of RegTech at ACA, also emphasised how financial crime’s dominance in RegTech adoption comes down to the high regulatory exposure. He noted that AML, KYC, sanctions and transaction monitoring combine high regulatory exposure with huge volumes of data and processes that are difficult to scale exclusively through people.

It has been revealed that the financial damages from a financial crime failure are also growing, making poor financial crime processes a major risk to a financial institution. On top of this, the cost of operations within managing financial crime requirements are also increasing rapidly, especially for firms operating across multiple jurisdictions.

A 2024 report from BioCatch claimed that 58% of organisations said they spent between $5m and $25m in operational costs related to investigating, combating and rectifying financial crime issues in 2023.

Recent findings suggest that another reason why Carciente believes the financial crime domain has seen strong adoption comes to the fact the business case is easy to demonstrate. He stated, “Firms can measure alert volumes, false positives, investigation times and manual review effort. When technology improves those metrics, the impact is visible.

“That hasn’t always been true elsewhere in compliance. Regulatory pressure by itself doesn’t guarantee technology adoption. In my experience, adoption moves fastest when the risk is material, the existing process doesn’t scale and there is a measurable benefit from changing it. Financial crime reached that point earlier than most other compliance disciplines.”.

Industry observers note that relyComply noted that the adoption of RegTech within financial crime is a response to the growing sophistication coming from the criminal underworld. “From exploiting the rise of digital assets, to employing crypto scams and using dark-web identity marketplaces to commit synthetic identity fraud, the underbelly is looking bleaker by the day.

“Add on the fact that regulatory concerns are created in response to this activity, it is perhaps unsurprising (and welcome) that retail and investment banks, payments firms and FinTechs are treating areas such as money laundering and terrorist financing with greater importance. Each form of crime puts their customers at risk – so too their regulatory reputations, where failing anti-fincrime controls could cause irreparable damage.”.

At the other end of the adoption index is the ESG domain, which has the lowest index score of 40. This domain covers solutions facilitating the measurement, management and reporting of environmental, social and governance factors for sustainable investing and corporate responsibility. Subcategories in this domain include ESG ratings, ESG reporting and disclosures, ESG risk management and climate risk quantification.

The Compliance Management domain has the second lowest adoption in compliance, with an Index score of 44. This domain refers to solutions helping to address regulatory challenges across multiple domains and frameworks. These are tools that help proactively manage regulatory obligations, changes and assurance activities across the enterprise. Subcategories include regulatory change management, regulatory intelligence, compliance assessment and compliance orchestration.

It has been revealed that much like how Sarda believes financial crime has led the charge with adoption due to the immediate and visible failures, compliance management and ESG lack this. Sarda said, “The obligations are broad rather than specific, failure is diffuse and slow to surface, and there’s no clean way to demonstrate the tool worked. That isn’t a reflection of the software. It’s a reflection of what firms can justify spending on.”.

Another reason why financial crime solutions have been popular could be down to the age of obligation. Copla CEO Aurimas Bakas stated, “Adoption tracks the age of the obligation and how clearly it names someone accountable. Financial crime has decades of prescriptive rules behind it, a public enforcement record with named penalties, and in the UK a statutory owner in the MLRO.

Industry observers note that “A vendor market has had time to mature against requirements that hold still. Information and technology security at 61 follows the same pattern, with data protection law, established security standards and a long incident history to build against.”.

It has been revealed that as such, the domains that are newly introduced obligations have lower adoption scores, Bakas said. For instance, the Resilience domain has an Index score of 46. The Resilience category covers solutions that ensure the continuity of critical business services by enabling firms to prepare for, respond to and recover from disruptions across operational and third-party environments. It includes the subcategories AI governance, third-party risk, incident response and enterprise risk management.

Recent findings suggest that third party risk only became a live submission requirement in the EU through the recently deployed DORA that requires the register of information. Similarly, the UK equivalent for material third-party arrangements is only coming through with PS26/2. AI governance is even newer.

In a recent development, the leading adopters for resilience are retail banks and investment banks, which Bakas noted are the institutions under the closest supervision. “When we speak to firms, movement on third party risk tends to begin with a supervisory conversation or a correction on a submitted register. Adoption there is following supervisory pressure, and the score reflects how recently that pressure arrived.”.

Why is there still relatively modest adoption.

The RegTech sector is no longer a niche market. The global RegTech market size is estimated to be worth $29.3bn in 2026. It is also projected to grow rapidly and become valued at $112.1bn by 2033. Despite the size of the market, the scores on the adoption index suggest the adoption of RegTech solutions is still rather modest across financial services.

Bakas noted that the modest nature of adoption is simply because the tools are coming quicker to the market than the regulations are. He stated, “Investment into the sector is running ahead of the obligations that pull tools into production. Firms commit when a requirement has a deadline, a named owner and a defined data format. Several categories in this taxonomy have the first two and are still waiting on the third.”.

Industry observers note that sarda noted that the adoption index does not necessarily suggest that financial institutions are not working with tools in these domains. The index score captures the depth of the adoption, and not whether the firm has bought a solution. Most of the firm, Sarda explained, have bought something in each of the six RegTech domains highlighted in the report. However, they are not running them independently.

“What they haven’t done is let it run the process unsupervised. Adoption stalls at parallel running, where the tool is live but a person still owns every decision, and it stays there until the buyer can defend the output to a regulator. Twenty years of AML enforcement has produced a score of 68 in the most mature domain. That tells you how slowly the ceiling moves in supervised environments.”.

The modest adoption is not a result of a shortage of technology, Carciente stated. Instead, the challenge is the environment the technology needs to adapt to. Most large financial institutions have built their systems over decades, with latest tools slowly embraced, typically in isolation. One team handles employee compliance, another does communications surveillance, another manages trade surveillance, etc.

Adding to the complexity is the various types of tools, whether it is an internal application, outsourced process or manual workflow.

Industry observers note that “Individually, these systems may work well. The difficulty comes when firms try to connect them.

In a recent development, “Data is fragmented, context gets lost between systems and integrations become expensive to maintain. We increasingly hear firms questioning whether they need another point solution or whether they need to simplify and connect what they already have.”.

This is unfortunately changing the RegTech adoption conversation. Instead of being whether the tool can solve a compliance problem, it is more focused on whether it can fit into the overall compliance operating model.

“I think that shift will shape the next three to five years. AI will accelerate it, but it won’t remove accountability.”.

Sources indicate that despite there being modest adoption within RegTech, RelyComply emphasised there is more that needs to be done. There are still major institutions failing to file timely reports, conduct continuous monitoring or handle risk-based onboarding.

RelyComply sees two areas need to change to support greater adoption of RegTech solutions.

Sources indicate that they remarked, “Fixing the lack of internal human skill and ownership culture, for one. The second is more material: addressing budget concerns under the dreaded ‘cost of compliance’ alongside legacy technology that’s causing disparate data, and AML and fraud workflows before any real-time systems can be integrated.”.

One challenge RelyComply pointed out was the need for a breakdown of silos.

Firms need to ensure there is some level of governance to ensure tools don’t overlap and cause confusion across compliance teams. While teams might look to certain RegTech tools that can solve unique solutions, they need to ensure ownership does not become blurred.

RelyComply stated that if one reporting engine is selected and another generative AI tool is used to collate auto-fill suspicious activity reports, it is bound to create mix-ups around who is responsible for overseeing the processes, utilising the same quality data as a different member that is tracking a different active investigation.

Industry observers note that “AI-based platforms may feel like a band-aid to automate everything. Yet there is no guarantee multiple tools can be engineered to suit a one-view AML, fraud and risk effort at all. Not to mention a stifled skills gap when AI implementations (handled in-house or through third parties) require not just advanced expertise, but a documented initiative for how AI models can be audited by an institution’s compliance teams.”.

Where will adoption grow the fastest?

The areas that will see the greatest adoption over the coming years is open for debate. AiPrise’s Sarda believes the mandates will be the defining factor behind which domains see the most adoption, rather than enthusiasm for the tools.

In a recent development, with that in mind, Sarda expects the Resilience domain will have a greater adoption Index score in the next five years, largely due to the AI governance space growing. The EU AI Act, for instance, will help to give the space a named obligation and deadline to adhere to, and other countries around the world are all set for similar rules around AI in the coming years.

Industry observers note that bakas also considered resilience as one of the domains that will see greater adoption levels in the coming years. He remarked, “In the EU, DORA made the register of information an annual submission with a defined data model, and the first cycle produced corrections that showed firms precisely where their data was weak. In the UK, PS26/2 brings reporting of material third party arrangements into scope, which gives firms a format to build toward.”.

In a similar vein, Carciente also pointed to the rising prominence of AI governance also increasing the adoption of resilience solutions in the coming years. As AI models become more integral to the running of business services, whether from internal models or the third-party dependence, they pose a major risk for firms.

Industry observers note that “As financial institutions deploy more AI, they need to know where it is being used, what data it accesses, what decisions it influences and what controls and oversight are required. AI won’t just change RegTech; it creates a new category of risk that firms need to govern.”.

Carciente pointed to a couple of other domains that could see strong adoption in the next few years. Surveillance tools are prime for this adoption growth. Carciente explained that firms must now monitor risk through email, messaging, voice, mobile communications and trading activity. This is becoming unsustainable through manual workloads and AI is helping to reduce noise and identify across the various datasets rather than creating false alerts or missing signals.

Industry observers note that carciente also sees regulatory change management generating a lot of adoption in the coming years, spurred on by the increased pace of change. There is an unprecedented level of regulatory change and firms have to monitor multiple updates and changes each week, not only understanding the changes but whether they are affected and what measures they need to take.

The underlying theme of RegTech adoption in the coming years, Carciente noted, will be the shift from disconnected applications towards more integrated compliance environments.

According to recent reports, “The first phase of RegTech largely digitized individual compliance processes. The next phase will be about connecting them: common data, connected workflows, case management, evidence and AI working across that information without losing context.”.

“Ultimately, financial crime’s lead isn’t an anomaly. It demonstrates what happens when regulatory risk, operational scale and measurable value come together. Other areas of compliance are now reaching similar pressure points. Teams are managing more data, more channels, more regulatory change and now more AI, often without significantly more resources.

“For me, that is where the next stage of RegTech becomes interesting. It won’t simply be about adding more automation or more tools. It will be about connecting data and workflows and using AI within a framework that firms can govern and defend. Financial crime revealed how technology can fundamentally change a compliance function. The opportunity now is to apply those lessons more broadly.”.
