Introducing BioBasicsBench, a benchmark measuring how well AI agents interpret raw experimental data across 100 routine wet-lab tasks.

Recent findings suggest that our mission is to ensure that artificial general intelligence benefits all of humanity. As part of that mission, we want to help scientists accelerate biological research—from understanding disease to developing latest treatments. For AI agents to contribute, they need to reliably interpret the experimental evidence scientists work with every day in wet labs, where they run experiments with biological samples. Everyday wet-lab work involves failed experiments, ambiguous images and conflicting observations. Existing benchmarks are built around charts and images selected for scientific papers may miss this ambiguity and the troubleshooting it requires. That’s why we’re introducing BioBasicsBench, a new benchmark developed with Ligo, a biotech tech giant focused on enzyme design, to measure how well agents handle routine wet-lab analysis.

Recent findings suggest that the benchmark’s 100 tasks test whether agents can interpret raw experimental data, identify errors, and recognize when the evidence cannot support a conclusion. Getting these tasks right matters because misreading an image or overlooking a problem with the controls used to check an experiment’s results can lead scientists to draw the wrong conclusion and spend time on experiments that won’t help answer their question.

At the ‘xhigh’ setting, which allows more reasoning per task, GPT‑6 Astra earned an average of 66.7% of the available credit, compared with 43.8% for GPT‑5.6 Sol. The results show progress on routine lab analysis, with room for further improvements in interpreting images, checking controls and applying experimental context.

BioBasicsBench uses raw experimental evidence, from gel images and colony plates to microscopy and instrument readouts.

BioBasicsBench has 100 tasks across five areas of routine wet-lab work: protein methods, imaging and cytometry, quantification and assays, nucleic acid methods, and molecular cloning. Tasks range from simple requests, such as reading the molecular weight of a DNA sample in a gel, to detailed assay analysis, but all involve routine work that wet-lab scientists should be able to complete reliably.

It has been revealed that scientists created the tasks using data from their own experiments, pairing each question with expert-written grading criteria. Each task underwent an internal review by a Ligo scientist who had not written it, an external review by a PhD-level scientist working in a wet lab, and a final internal review.

Sources indicate that agents return a structured answer with the requested conclusion, measurement or recommendation and, where required, supporting reasoning. Answers receive credit against individual rubric criteria, allowing the benchmark to distinguish a correct conclusion from a partially correct analysis. Most tasks test several skills together, including biological knowledge, interpretation of images and other data, error detection and protocol understanding.

It has been revealed that we evaluated seven models on BioBasicsBench: GPT‑5.2, GPT‑5.4, GPT‑5.5, GPT‑5.6 Sol, GPT‑6.1 Sol, GPT‑6 Luna and GPT‑6 Astra. The evaluation covers 37 model and reasoning-effort settings, with five attempts on each of the 100 tasks per setting, for a total of 18,500 results.

Scientific research is highly multimodal, requiring constant visual interpretation and decision making. Astra displayed a significant improvement at handling multimodal data which was given in the form of photos and videos, as well as raw outputs from sequencing, plate-reader assays, microscopy and flow cytometry.

Recent findings suggest that an inaccurate colony count directly affects estimates of how efficiently DNA was introduced into cells. With the plated volume and dilution held fixed, an overcount inflates that estimate by the same proportion.

Accelerating biological research requires getting the everyday work right. BioBasicsBench makes that work a target for improvement: interpreting raw experimental data, checking controls and recognizing when a conclusion is not supported. Progress on these tasks could help scientists spend less time troubleshooting and more time deciding which experiments to pursue next. Tracking these capabilities will also help us understand their implications for biosafety and biosecurity.
