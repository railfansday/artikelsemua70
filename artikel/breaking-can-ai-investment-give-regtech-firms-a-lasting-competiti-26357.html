Breaking: Can AI investment give RegTech firms a lasting competitive edge?

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

It has been revealed that regTech firms are investing heavily in AI, but the scale of that investment alone will not determine who comes out ahead. As AI capabilities become increasingly accessible, the competitive advantage will lie in how effectively firms translate them into better products, stronger customer outcomes and more differentiated offerings.

Recent findings suggest that regTech Analyst and Parker & Lawrence Research recently published a major industry report examining the global state of RegTech in 2026. As part of their findings, they looked at the return on investment on the commercial benefits of AI, and how this differed based on business size.

FinTech Global recently sat down with several leading players in RegTech to get their thoughts on these findings in a second part of a two-part series.

Why are some firms in the market generating significantly higher returns from AI than others?

In the view of Sarah French, CRO of TaxTec, the difference often comes down to whether organisations are investing in technology to solve a clearly defined business problem or simply investing in AI because they feel they should.

She remarked, “There is a significant difference between introducing automation and fundamentally improving how an operation functions.”.

It has been revealed that “For example, in financial services, many processes remain dependent on manual investigation, fragmented information, specialist knowledge and multiple operational touchpoints. Automating individual tasks may deliver incremental efficiencies, but the greater value comes from looking at the entire operating model and identifying where complexity can be removed.”.

For French, organisations that understand their operational challenges, establish clear objectives and measure outcomes are better positioned to realise meaningful returns from their technology investments.

Meanwhile, Areg Nzsdejan, CEO of Cardamon, remarked that the interesting thing for him in the survey was how little separates the groups of reduced manual effort.

According to recent reports, he remarked, “50.6% of the highest-ROI group report it, compared with 47.5% of the lowest. The gaps are much wider for faster onboarding, approvals or reviews, at 43.4% versus 27.5%, and faster product launches or market expansion, at 18.1% versus 2.5%.”.

These, he makes clear, are reported benefits, so the figures do not establish what caused the higher returns.

For the Cardamon CEO, however, they do suggest a useful question: has the time saved changed what the business can do?

“Automating a task creates limited commercial value if the same approval queue still holds everything up,” he stresses.

In the view of Anthony Quinn, CEO of Arctic Intelligence, the organisations who are seeing real returns from AI aren’t the ones automating the most tasks.

“They’re the ones who’ve tied AI to a specific customer outcome, such as faster, more defensible risk decisions, and built it into the product rather than bolting it on,” he explained.

In the view of Andrew Davies, global head of FCC strategy at ComplyAdvantage, the gap usually comes down to how the investment was scoped, rather than which model was selected.

He explained, “In our 2026 State of Financial Crime Report, 48% of compliance leaders reported that their firms have dedicated projects and budgets for AI in financial crime. Almost as many (46%) reported that their AI budget is confined to specific teams or siloed parts of the technology stack. Ultimately, an investment scoped to a single function returns value to only that function.”.

For Davies, there is a second, less comfortable explanation. Almost nine in ten respondents – 88% – admitted that including AI in a proposed compliance modernization program increases the likelihood of gaining approval and securing resources.

He remarked, “AI is simply easier to fund than most compliance technology, which means some programs are approved on the strength of the “AI” label rather than the viability of the business case. Firms that defined their desired outcomes first – such as fewer false positives, faster remediation, or better risk scoring – have something to measure returns against. Firms that funded the technology first simply do not.”.

How can RegTech firms move beyond automation to drive growth and commercial value?

In the opinion of Davies, buyers are already making that shift. He highlighted how when asked about the outcomes they have seen or expect from agentic and predictive AI, respondents named increased efficiency first at 54%, followed by improved customer experience and satisfaction at 51% – significantly ahead of cost reduction at 26%.

He commented, “The commercial case for AI in compliance is being built on driving growth rather than just savings: faster onboarding, fewer legitimate customers held in review, and less friction between a firm and its revenue.”.

For the FCC head, the real opportunity lies in the work that companies have not yet automated.

Davies stated, “Auto-remediation of level-one alerts is at the moment the least-implemented capability at 32%, behind suspicious activity detection at 55% and transaction monitoring at 53%. And while 95% of compliance leaders agree automation of routine work would be highly helpful, not a single respondent reported already having that capability – an unmet need in the highest-volume, lowest-judgment part of the workflow.”.

Meanwhile, for Nzsdejan, he suggested firms should start with a business decision that compliance work is delaying.

“For a latest product or market, a firm needs to establish which obligations apply, what it already covers and what has to change. At Cardamon, we speed up that assessment by mapping regulatory obligations against existing policies and controls, helping teams identify gaps and the work needed to close them earlier,” he said.

With this, firms can reduce compliance delays to a product launch or entry into a latest market, while keeping the same standard of review.

In a recent development, he added, “RegTech providers should measure the whole process. Time to an approved decision, rework and cost per completed assessment are more useful than the number of documents summarised.

Recent findings suggest that “For the provider, reusable workflows can also reduce implementation costs and make it economical to serve more customers. That advantage depends on how much specialist work is still needed for each deployment.”.

Quinn, however, is succinct, “Automation cuts cost, but it’s a race to the bottom. Commercial value comes from using AI to deliver insight customers couldn’t get before.”.

For French, the opportunity extends well beyond reducing processing time or operational costs.

“Technology can enable financial institutions to deliver services that would previously have been commercially difficult to provide, improve the experience for their clients and scale operations without a corresponding increase in resources,” she mentioned.

Recent findings suggest that french mentioned that withholding tax recovery is a good example. “Historically, the operational effort involved in identifying, documenting, submitting and tracking reclaims has created barriers to delivering these services efficiently. Technology can help change that equation by centralising information, improving workflow management, reducing manual intervention and providing greater transparency over recovery opportunities.”.

In a recent development, the commercial value, she added, is not simply in doing the same work faster. It is enabling institutions to identify additional opportunities, improve service delivery and make previously resource-intensive activities commercially viable.

She went on, “AI may accelerate this further, but its value needs to be measured against tangible business outcomes rather than the sophistication of the technology itself.”.

What separates firms that successfully scale AI from those struggling to realise its potential?

The firms that struggle to scale tend to treat AI as a project, noted Quinn. The ones that succeed, he believes, treat it as an operating model, with clean data, clear governance and a roadmap tied to revenue.

On the other hand, French made clear her view that successful organisations recognise that technology adoption is as much an operational and strategic challenge as it is a technical one.

She said, “Scalability requires reliable data, clear processes, effective governance and integration with the wider technology environment.”.

Recent findings suggest that in addition, it also requires an understanding of where human expertise remains vital. For example, inhighly specialised areas such as tax and regulatory operations, technology should support experienced professionals by removing repetitive activities and improving access to information, rather than assuming every decision can or should be automated.

She commented, “The organisations that succeed will be those that combine technological capability with genuine subject-matter expertise and a clear understanding of their clients’ operating environments.

It has been revealed that “Ultimately, competitive advantage will not come from simply having AI. It will come from how effectively that technology translates into better outcomes for clients, stronger operational resilience and sustainable commercial growth.”.

Nszdejan believes that the scalers make it possible to repeat a good result.

According to recent reports, he explained, “A pilot can work because a few experts quietly correct the output. Scaling requires those corrections to become test cases, clear review rules and improvements to the underlying workflow.

“Teams need realistic examples of both routine and difficult work, and they need to test again when the model or source material changes. Someone must own the process and decide when an uncertain answer goes to a person.”.

Sources indicate that the economics for the Cardamon CEO should involve ongoing maintenance. If each new customer requires another layer of manual checking, the cost of delivery will keep rising with revenue, he stated.

Sources indicate that in the view of Davies, the bar has moved, and the regulator is helping set it. Speaking at the Bloomberg UK Policy Series on October 19, 2026, Jessica Rusu, the Financial Conduct Authority’s Chief Data, Information and Intelligence Officer, outlined the FCA’s vision for an “agentic regulator”.

Recent findings suggest that the watchdog is developing its own agentic tools to automate supervision, with initial deployments monitoring around 9,000 smaller wholesale firms filing transaction reports. It is also preparing to oversee the anti-money laundering controls of a further 60,000 firms expected to enter its remit from 2028.

According to Davies, the significance extends beyond the scale of these operations. As regulators themselves adopt AI to strengthen supervision, firms face growing pressure to demonstrate that they can monitor, explain and assure how their own systems behave in production.

Industry observers note that AI governance can no longer be treated as a periodic exercise. It must become a continuous function, embedded throughout the deployment lifecycle. This is increasingly the dividing line between firms scaling AI successfully and those struggling to move beyond pilots.

Sources indicate that however, for Davies, the biggest obstacle is often not the technology itself, but the architecture supporting it. Organisations operate an average of seven separate systems for each compliance activity, spanning customer screening, transaction monitoring, case management and regulatory reporting.

Deploying AI across this fragmented infrastructure risks reproducing the very data gaps, inconsistencies and operational blind spots that firms are trying to eliminate.

Adoption figures illustrate the gap between ambition and execution. For standard AI in customer screening, 93% of firms are using, piloting or evaluating a vendor solution, with 62% having successfully moved into production. Agentic AI presents a different picture. Although 73% are using, piloting or evaluating the technology, just 33% have reached production.

Davies believes that successful AI deployment depends on more than selecting the right model. Firms must first establish the data foundations, system connectivity and governance needed to support it. Increasingly, these are not simply prerequisites for scaling AI. They are also essential to keeping pace with a regulator that is becoming more technologically capable itself.

Davies concluded, “The firms seeing real returns from AI didn’t start with the model. They started by defining what a good outcome looks like, then ensuring they could measure it continuously.

Sources indicate that “This is no longer just good practice – when the regulator is running its own agentic tools, a system that cannot demonstrate how it behaves in production becomes a liability rather than an advantage. Almost every firm expects AI to deliver. Far fewer have built the data foundation that allows it to do so.”.
