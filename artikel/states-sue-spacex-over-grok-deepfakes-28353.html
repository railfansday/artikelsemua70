States Sue SpaceX Over Grok Deepfakes

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Industry observers note that the attorneys general of Oregon and Virginia are suing Elon Musk’s SpaceX, alleging that the manufacturer violated consumer protection laws by lying to users about both the safety of its generative AI tools and how seriously the company took stopping child sexual abuse material (CSAM) and nonconsensual intimate imagery from proliferating on its platforms. The suits follow a slew of other lawsuits against Musk’s companies around similar problems, including from the city of Baltimore and teens in Tennessee.

Recent findings suggest that the attorneys general are each filing their own complaints in their respective state courts—Virginia in the circuit court for the city of Richmond, and Oregon in Multnomah County circuit court. Both suits name SpaceX and X Corp., the SpaceX subsidiary responsible for the social media platform X. SpaceX acquired xAI, the Musk startup and parent company of X Corp., in February. In doing so it also took ownership of Grok, which is at the center of the allegations in the suit.

Industry observers note that last winter, the Grok chatbot made headlines after customers on X started prompting it en masse to generate sexualized photos of women and minors. Even more disturbing AI-generated videos were later found on Grok’s website, including those showing female celebrities engaging in sexual activities.

The states allege that Grok’s creators ignored industry safety standards and intentionally implemented weak safeguards in an attempt to entice more people to use its “lagging product.” In all, as Virginia alleged in its complaint, the defendants saw “a market opportunity for more profits” in the generation of sexualized images of real people, including children, and “intentionally released a product that inundated their platforms with CSAM.”.

For example, the states say that Grok wasn’t subjected to enough third-party evaluations before releasing new models and that Grok’s safety prompts included instructions to “assume good intent” and that the terms “teenage” and “girl” do “not necessarily imply underage.” They also allege that tens of thousands of reports were submitted without key information from xAI to the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children’s CyberTipline, preventing the Center from being able to act on them 90 percent of the time.

It has been revealed that in the complaints, the states say that Musk leaned into Grok’s “spicy mode” as a way to differentiate the model from its competitors and that this worked. They note, for example, that during the same time period that Grok’s ability to “undress” people was sparking international outrage, Musk posted about how Grok was “hitting #1 on the App Store in one country after another!” and discussing the chatbot’s “heavy usage growth.”.

“It’s no surprise that Grok and X became a haven for perpetrators using AI to create and share child sexual abuse material, because the lack of needed safety measures was considered a feature, not a bug,” states Oregon attorney general Dan Rayfield in a statement.

Industry observers note that oregon and Virginia each filed their own “separate but similar actions” on Monday, but say they’ve been working in collaboration with each other. Both states retained Platkin LLP, a private law firm run by former Latest Jersey attorney general Matthew Platkin, to help litigate their cases.

Industry observers note that virginia attorney general Jay Jones says that both the collaboration and the decision to work with an outside firm came from trying to make the most of what they had. “Our offices are strapped. I wish I had all the resources in the world,” says Jones. “And so we oftentimes do partner with folks on the outside to make sure that we’re pursuing the matters that are in the best interest of our constituencies.”.

Recent findings suggest that “I have a deep worry now into the future with this case and many others that we actually don’t have the appropriate tools to deal with trillion-dollar corporations,” says Jones. “This is just a line item on a budget as the cost of doing business, right? Dealing with, you know, AG Jones and AG Rayfield. I think it’s going to be real interesting to see how these cases go, because it’s not just this, there’s a lot of other things that we’re looking at. How is this going to be grappled with as a society into the future?”.

In its complaint, Virginia states that shortly before filing, its investigators tested Grok and found its image-generation safeguards were not only “inconsistent and ineffective” but also taught consumers how to bypass moderation measures by explaining which words triggered its filters. When asked, the chatbot also helped state investigators draft prompts designed to avoid moderation and then carried out those prompts on request.

“At no point,” Virginia’s complaint indicates, “did Grok ask whether (a) the image uploaded was of a real person, or (b) whether the user had the person’s consent to use or modify the photo.”.

Both Virginia and Oregon say that this contradicts assurances made by a SpaceX official during a New York City Council hearing this October.

The attorneys general are seeking civil penalties and attorneys’ fees, but also pointed to Meta’s settling—without admitting wrongdoing—this summer with 51 US states and territories over alleged failures to protect minors as an example of how enforcers can demand that companies make sweeping product changes to resolve consumer protection cases.

Industry observers note that “They profited off the backs of children with these types of images to get a strategic advantage, and it worked. You don’t get to benefit from that type of stuff; there has to be an associated fee and penalty just like anything else,” claims Dan Rayfield.

Adds Jones, “Your size and your scope shouldn’t excuse the fact that you have to play by the rules.”.

In response to a request for comment, SpaceX directed WIRED to a post on X’s official safety account. “Protecting children is non-negotiable,” the account wrote. “It has no place on X or in Grok, and neither do explicit images of real people made without their consent.”.

In a recent development, the post further went on to say that Grok’s designers continue “testing and adjusting” protections designed to refuse requests to generate explicit images of real people or minors. SpaceX’s subsidiary, xAI, has previously argued in court that it takes violations of its terms of service seriously, touting a lawsuit it filed against a South Carolina user for alleged breach of contract after he was arrested for possessing CSAM, including some allegedly generated using Grok—one of several similar lawsuits the firm has filed. Both xAI’s lawsuit against the individual and his criminal case are still ongoing.
