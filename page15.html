It has been revealed that the format depends on what you’re asking. A comparison might work best side by side. An explanation might be easier to understand through an interactive diagram. And when a simple text answer is the most useful response, ChatGPT can give you that.

You can also ask ChatGPT to create a tool for the task at hand, such as a calculator to explore how savings could grow, a bill splitter for dinner with friends, or a game you can play in the conversation.

With Intelligent UI, we gave GPT‑6 the freedom to create the most useful interface for each question, while keeping the experience fast and familiar across web and mobile.

Industry observers note that we built a library of native, streamable components, along with a compiler that processes the interface as the model generates it. The library gives each response a familiar design foundation while leaving the model room to decide how the pieces fit together. The compiler allows the interface to appear progressively as the model generates it, without waiting for the entire response to be complete.

We expanded our training methods to help the model make thoughtful decisions about content, layout, visuals, and interaction. This included evaluating the interfaces it creates for clarity, usefulness, and completeness. GPT‑6 learned to use the component library and make good design decisions, including how to organize information clearly, when to use interactivity, and when a simple text response is enough.

There's still work ahead to improve the model's design judgment and expand what it can create, but Intelligent UI marks a significant shift in how people interact with ChatGPT. Instead of fitting every question into the same conversational format, ChatGPT can combine UI, data, and actions to create entirely new ways for people to explore, understand, and interact with information.

With GPT‑6, we’re also making it possible for ChatGPT to begin answering while it continues to think.

With the introduction of reasoning models, consumers could get help with much harder problems, but had to wait for the model to finish thinking before getting an answer. Now, ChatGPT can interleave thinking with answering. We trained it to take into account the user’s waiting time and progressively compose an answer using its knowledge and findings up to that point. It learned to build answers across multiple partial responses, each adding useful information without unnecessary filler, while keeping the full answer as cohesive and factual as one written all at once.

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

College Planner is coming to ChatGPT for teens, alongside a upcoming teen AI council and more tools for learning.

Since we introduced ChatGPT for Teens—the default experience automatically applied to accounts identified as under 18—we’ve seen young people use it to learn, explore ideas and solve everyday problems. Today, we’re sharing early progress on ChatGPT for Teens, including how teens are using its learning tools, along with new ways for teens to study and prepare for college.

Industry observers note that chatGPT’s 1.2 billion consumers span many countries, communities and backgrounds. That reach makes it especially important to build tools that help young people with different resources and opportunities learn and prepare for their futures.

Industry observers note that chatGPT for Teens is designed to help young people learn, think critically and deepen their understanding. As ChatGPT for Teens became available to more teens globally, those with access sent about 2.7 million more learning-related messages on average than those who didn’t have it yet.

In a recent development, once enrolled, teens use ChatGPT for Teens’ learning tools at scale. The most selected conversation starter prompts are “ask questions to develop my ideas,” “give feedback on my draft,” and “help me check my evidence.” In one week, nearly 1.2 million teens used Learning Visualizations to better understand math and science concepts, and more than 180,000 used Study Mode for step-by-step learning support.

ChatGPT for Teens includes built-in reminders to encourage teens to take breaks. On average, teens spend under 15 minutes a day on ChatGPT. While less than 2% of teens spend more than three consecutive hours on ChatGPT, in almost half of teen conversations with a break reminder, teens took a break or ended their conversation within five minutes. These usage patterns remain well below the publicly reported⁠(opens in a latest window) average of 5 hours for teen social media usage, and reflect a different kind of experience: ChatGPT is designed for learning, building, and creativity, rather than passive scrolling. Among teens with sessions longer than three consecutive hours, over 80% had at least one prompt related to learning.

These early signs are encouraging and we’ll continue to evaluate the experience and learn from teens, families and educators to improve ChatGPT for Teens.

Hundreds of thousands of US teens use ChatGPT each week to blueprint for college. They’re using it to plan applications, track deadlines, research schools and understand financial aid.

That’s why we’ll soon be adding College Planner to ChatGPT for Teens. We’re designing it to help students manage the practical steps of applying to college, without taking over the personal work of figuring out where they want to apply. Students have different levels of access to support with college applications. Our goal is to make help more accessible, regardless of financial circumstances, alongside guidance from counselors, advisers, and families.

In a recent development, college Planner brings together application requirements, deadlines, tasks, and financial-aid steps for schools on a student’s list in one plan. Students applying to several colleges can return to it to enhancement their progress, use the timeline to plan ahead and see what’s left to do. They can also ask ChatGPT questions along the way, from what a requirement means to how to get started. By bringing these steps together, we aim to save students and families time and make a stressful process feel more manageable.

We’re designing the initial US experience for high-school students in grades 10–12 who are planning to attend a four-year college. We plan to bring College Planner to students in more countries and expand support to two-year colleges, technical colleges, trade schools and more over time.

Sources indicate that college Planner also helps students figure out how to pay for college. They can get help understanding financial aid requirements, track each school’s aid deadlines, and explore scholarships and fee waivers.

Keeping track of an application is only one part of preparing for college. Many students also need someone to help them make sense of their options. Alongside College Planner, we’re supporting College Advising Corps⁠(opens in a new window), a nonprofit that places recent college graduates as advisers in under-resourced high schools to help students navigate the path to higher education. OpenAI’s contribution will help the organization expand into new states and create an AI fellowship program. Through the fellowship, advisers across participating states will test and share practical ways to use AI to help more students navigate the path to college. AI can help students manage application and enrollment tasks. Advisers can help them consider their options and decide what to do next.

When we introduced ChatGPT for Teens, we committed to keep improving the experience. We’re supporting Boston Children’s Hospital’s Digital Wellness Lab⁠(opens in a latest window) and its independent Student Advisory Council⁠(opens in a new window) over the next three years to learn directly from young people about how AI fits into their lives and to give teenagers a meaningful stake at the table as AI is being designed.

For the 2026–27 school year, the Lab will bring together ~22 students across two tracks: ~12 students ages 14–18 working on digital wellbeing projects in their communities and ~10 students ages 16–20, focused on building AI chatbots and emerging technology. These students will work alongside researchers and clinicians on digital wellbeing and AI projects. Students in the AI track will test tools and recommend changes based on how they use AI in learning and their personal lives. We expect to seek feedback on teen safety defaults, parental controls and notifications and AI literacy.

Teens should play a central role in developing the tools that will shape their future. Teens will test tools and develop recommendations for the people building them. Our aim is to bring those recommendations into product and model decisions early and to report back to students on what we change and why. This advisory board will give them a direct voice in that work and share their insights and recommendations to help the wider industry build better tools for young people.

Sources indicate that we heard from students that photographing and uploading their notes to ChatGPT can take hours. We’re making it easier for students to turn their notes into study materials like flashcards and quizzes.

Industry observers note that with continuous, multi-photo capture, students can photograph multiple pages of notes and automatically combine them into a single PDF, ready to upload to ChatGPT. This is available on iOS, and we’re working to bring it to Android.

We’re also adding flashcards to help teens practice what they’re learning. A student preparing for a biology test, for example, can upload class notes or choose a topic, and ChatGPT can turn the material into cards to review. They can mark the cards they know, return to the ones they missed or shuffle the deck for another round. Decks are saved in a teen’s Library, so it’s easy to pick up where they left off. Students can also ask ChatGPT to pull up the flashcards and create a scheduled task to practice them.

Alongside flashcards, we’ve made it easier to create Quizzes in ChatGPT. Students can now upload their notes to turn them into interactive quizzes, and ChatGPT is better at recognizing when you want a quiz and creating one directly in chat.

We’ll keep learning from teens, families and educators, checking how protections work in practice and sharing what we learn as ChatGPT for Teens evolves.

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

According to recent reports, over the past two and a half years, we’ve reported on many of the ways in which threat actors tried to use our models to conduct cyber attacks, covert influence operations (IO), scams, and other violations of our Usage Policies. We publish these reports to inform regulators, our industry peers, and wider society of how we see threat actors trying to leverage AI, and to shine a light on the vulnerabilities they try to exploit.

We recently banned two IO—one from Russia and one from Iran—that used our models in combination with more traditional techniques and technologies to support sophisticated “false front” entities. They used those entities to launder geopolitical, conflict-related messaging into their target audiences. The Iranian operation included a stable of seven “journalist” personas which it used to pitch long-form articles to small and medium online outlets around the world; the Russian operation appears to have co-opted unwitting people in Latin America to run a “think tank” on the ground.

Both operations were multi-dimensional, trying a range of different tactics to achieve their goals. As well as long-form articles, the Iranian operation generated batches of social media comments, generally on topics related to the US-Iran war. As well as controlling the “think tank,” the Russian operation created fake “leaked” documents and audio scripts, some of which we identified being spread online. Both operations also made heavy use of AI to draft internal reports (the Russian operation did this more than anything else); in both cases, the actors used questionable or outright deceitful methodologies to exaggerate the operators’ effectiveness.

What is most striking about these operations is that they closely resembled complex influence operations of the pre-AI age, but used AI to make some of the workflows easier. The journalist personas run by the Iranian operation bore a family resemblance to the fake journalist “Alice⁠(opens in a latest window) Donovan⁠(opens in a new window),” a front for Russian military intelligence whose articles were published by a range of Western outlets in 2016-17. In 2020, individuals associated with past activity by the Russian Internet Research Agency ran a fake “news” outlet called “PeaceData⁠(opens in a new window),” which co-opted unwitting journalists around the world into writing for it.

In a recent development, these operations were also unusual for their potential reach. Using the IO Breakout Scale⁠(opens in a new window), which rates IO on a scale of 1 (lowest) to 6 (highest), we would assess the Russia-origin operation as belonging in Category 5. This is the first Category 5 operation we’ve disrupted since we began our reporting. The Iran-origin operation reached Category 4. Both managed to land their content (not all of which was generated from our models) in mainstream media outlets, rather than simply posting it on social media. This is consistent with a pattern we’ve observed across the 30 covert influence operations we’ve exposed in the last two and a half years: the operations which try to land their content in real media outlets, rather than relying on fake social media distribution, tend to have the highest potential reach and impact.

Sources indicate that matrix of the 30 IO we’ve exposed since early 2024, assessed by primary distribution method (social media, operation-run website, external publications) and score on the Breakout Scale. Operations which used more than one distribution method, such as running a website and promoting it on social media, are classified by the method which appeared to constitute the core of the operation.

AI can give such false-front operations greater scale, efficiency, linguistic fluency, and editorial ability. The operators can use these advantages to exploit unsuspecting victims, such as employees or editors, and plant their content in front of audiences who have no idea who was behind it, or what their motivations were. These operations also demonstrate the complexity and sophistication of threat actors’ use of false personas and entities to achieve their goals. Understanding the vulnerabilities that threat actors exploit in these efforts, and how those vulnerabilities can translate into audience and reach, will be essential to maintaining robust defenses across organizations.

But the covert nature of these false-front operations also makes them particularly vulnerable to responsible disclosure. Both “Alice Donovan” and “PeaceData” ceased their activity after they were exposed. That’s why our goal in reporting these false-front operations is to make further research and disruption easier—and make continuing the operations harder.

Russia-origin actors running influence campaigns across Latin America.

We banned a cluster of ChatGPT accounts that originated in Russia. They used ChatGPT for a range of tasks associated with covert influence operations targeting countries across Latin America. Much of this activity appeared aimed at undermining Ukraine’s reputation in the region, but some appeared aimed at influencing local political outcomes, especially in Argentina and Bolivia. Most of the operators prompted in Russian; one prompted in Spanish, but nevertheless appeared to be located in Russia. We have shared information on this case with the relevant authorities.

The operators used ChatGPT to perform three main tasks: writing internal reports on their activities (and other people’s activities which they could plausibly take credit for), creating content for their operations, and drafting reports on the performance of a self-described “research platform” in Latin America called the Social Research Center (SRC). They appear to have controlled the SRC via a fake persona named “Mia Clark”; in honor of the name, we have nicknamed this operation “Dark Clark.” Since we do not allow access to our models from Russia, they used VPNs to connect to our services.

Sources indicate that in their internal reports, the operators claimed to have spread fake stories across Latin America to undermine Ukraine or local leaders. Some of these fakes have been attributed by open-source⁠(opens in a new window) researchers⁠(opens in a new window) to a Russian entity known as “Politology” or “La Compania,” a reported successor to the Wagner Group and other entities founded by Russian oligarch Yevgeniy Prigozhin. The operators also asked our models to translate or explain public reporting about Politology and Wagner, far more than asking about any other Russia-origin networks.

This operation was unusual in two ways. First, it appears to have successfully co-opted individuals in Latin America to work for the SRC. The Russian operators’ reports on the SRC referred to decisions over issues such as pay scales, hiring, and firing, suggesting that they controlled the entity, rather than cooperating with it. The available evidence indicates that the SRC’s employees in Latin America were not aware that they were working for a Russian group. Unlike another Russia-linked “think tank” that we recently exposed, the SRC appears to have produced a majority of original content via its co-opted staff. This stands out as the most complex attempt to run a front identity that we’ve disrupted over the past two and a half years.

Industry observers note that second, some open-source evidence suggests that Dark Clark’s fakes spread widely enough to provoke fact checks⁠(opens in a new window) and official denials⁠(opens in a new window), indicating a degree of penetration which goes beyond any of the influence operations we disrupted over the past two years. One fake that the operation claimed to have planted in Peru even fed into public tensions between Ukraine and Poland.

Recent findings suggest that the main way the operators used ChatGPT was to draft and revision internal reports to an unknown superior. These regular reports described efforts to conduct covert influence campaigns across Latin America. They encompassed three main workstreams: efforts to denigrate Ukraine and undermine recruitment for the Ukrainian Armed Forces; efforts to interfere in the domestic politics of certain countries, especially Bolivia and Argentina; and management of the SRC. In the majority of cases, we did not observe the threat actors using our models to create content for the campaigns, only to report on them (the few exceptions are described below⁠).

According to recent reports, the reports were rich in tactical detail, shining a light on how the operation sought to undermine Ukraine and some national leaders, notably the presidents of Argentina, Bolivia, and Ecuador. Some described efforts to trick locals into carrying out activities that the operators could weaponize; others described fake “leaks” that the operators claimed to have spread.

It has been revealed that in two cases, the evidence ties the operators to public reporting on “Politology.” The operators reported that, in 2024, they ran two campaigns targeting Argentina’s President Javier Milei. First, they mentioned they spread a false report that Milei had bought Cartier jeweled collars for his dogs. Second, they claimed that they paid local actors to post anti-Milei graffiti in Buenos Aires. Based on leaked documents, both these claims have been publicly⁠(opens in a newly introduced window) attributed⁠(opens in a new window) to “Politology” and “La Compania.”.

Industry observers note that many more fakes have not been previously tied to Russian IO. For example, the operators claimed that in May 2026, they created a fake email address purporting to come from the Regional Directorate of Education in Lima, Peru. They used this to instruct schools in the district to hold events dedicated to Ukraine on the national Day of Cultural and Linguistic Diversity (May 21). The emails included explicit instructions to reference, among others, controversial twentieth-century Ukrainian nationalist Stepan Bandera, who is seen by some Ukrainians as an independence figure, but associated in Russia and Poland with fascism, wartime collaboration, and atrocities. According to the operators, some schools replied to the fake email address, confirming that they had held such events and even providing pictures.

It has been revealed that the operators then claimed to have planted stories about the events in the media in both Peru and Poland, alongside allegations that Ukraine was “exporting” ultra-nationalist ideologies, triggering outrage. Open-source searches identified stories that matched this claim in the Peruvian⁠(opens in a upcoming window) and⁠(opens in a new window) Polish⁠(opens in a new window) press⁠(opens in a new window), and an English-language publication in Hungary⁠(opens in a new window) (some of the articles have since been deleted). Some⁠(opens in a new window) articles⁠(opens in a new window) included a reaction from a Polish Member of the European Parliament proposing that people who showed “anti-Polishness” be declared persona non grata in Poland. The Russian fake thus both fed on, and fed into, historical tensions between Ukrainians and Poles.

Similarly, in June, the operators claimed they used a different fake email address to trick schools in Ecuador into holding a ceremony pledging allegiance to President Daniel Noboa and to Erik Prince, former head of private military contractor Blackwater. The operators claimed that the incident provoked outrage in Ecuador and put pressure on the government to deny the fake, thus amplifying it to a nationwide audience. Again, open-source research identified media⁠(opens in a new window) coverage⁠(opens in a new window) in the Ecuadorian⁠(opens in a new window) press⁠(opens in a new window) that closely resembled this claim, and even a detailed rebuttal⁠(opens in a new window) by Ecuador’s Minister for Education.

The operators used a range of techniques to underpin their false stories. According to their internal reporting, they spread two different fakes targeting Ecuador in March. One used fake audio attributed to Ukraine’s consul in Ecuador, in which he was alleged to have made disparaging comments about Ecuadorians. Our open-source investigation identified a post on TikTok which matched this precise claim; it appeared to receive only limited engagement. The other described a fake video clip spread on X and TikTok under the logo of a genuine news channel, alleging that Noboa’s government was recruiting young men to fight in Ukraine. Fact checkers⁠(opens in a latest window) in Ecuador described a campaign that matched this description in early April.

Recent findings suggest that another internal report described a fake they spread in Bolivia in late May, at the height of anti-government protests. The operators described the goal of this campaign explicitly as to exacerbate the crisis around the protests. The campaign featured a fake audio recording of a worker at the state water firm, EPSAS, saying that the government was going to shut off water to the administrative capital, La Paz, and declare a state of emergency. Our open-source research identified a debunk⁠(opens in a new window) of the matching claim and an official denial⁠(opens in a new window) from EPSAS.

Other internal reports included fakes that we were unable to corroborate from open sources, possibly because they had already been taken down, or failed to spread. For example, the operators claimed to have spread a story in January alleging that a Brazilian influencer and model had been trafficked into Ukraine by representatives of the Ukrainian Embassy, and had been killed in Kyiv. In June, they claimed to have spread another fake audio clip in Bolivia, this time purporting to come from an employee of the Central Bank of Bolivia, warning that the Bank was about to limit cash withdrawals. They also claimed to have spread a faked letter from the country’s hydrocarbons agency, warning of restrictions on fuel sales to private cars.

In other reports, the operators asked our models to help identify incidents in the target countries that they could take credit for, even if they had not been involved. For example, in Argentina, the operators claimed that the Argentinian Foreign Minister had quoted their operation in a United Nations committee meeting on the Falkland / Malvinas islands. The operators took credit for seeding the arguments that the principle of self-determination does not apply to the islands, and that the UK maintains a disproportionate military presence there. In fact, both arguments have been part of Argentina’s official position for many⁠(opens in a newly introduced window) years⁠(opens in a new window). Similarly, in June, Brazilian media reported⁠(opens in a new window) on the case of a Brazilian citizen who had travelled to Ukraine to join the army, been captured by Russian forces, and then made a televised statement claiming that he had been tricked into fighting. The ChatGPT operators tried to claim that this report reproduced their operation’s messaging; in fact, it quoted the captive’s own video⁠(opens in a new window). This suggests an attempt by the operators to run an influence operation targeting their own employers, rather than any external audience.

Sources indicate that some of the operators’ reports referred to a self-styled “research platform” called the Social Research Center (in Russian, Центр социологических исследований). According to the SRC’s website, it focuses on the Indian diaspora in Latin America, and relations between India and the countries of the region.

The internal reports suggested that the operators controlled the think tank, rather than merely working with it. They referenced, for example, hiring and firing decisions, pay scales, staffing prepares, and ongoing research projects. Crucially, they also stated that the operators had created a fake persona, named “Mia Clark,” to run the operation’s social media activity and engage with staff on the ground in Latin America. The available evidence suggests that these on-the-ground employees had no idea that they were working for a Russian operation, and conducted research tasks in good faith. This use of unwitting cutouts strongly resembles the earlier Russian operation also linked to Prigozhin’s world, “PeaceData,” which Meta exposed⁠(opens in a upcoming window) in 2020.

To judge by the Russian operators’ reports, their staff on the ground in Latin America conducted interviews with experts and commentators across the region, and then drafted research papers based on their findings. The topics ranged from a broad analysis of public opinion on the BRICS group, to a detailed comparison of the Brazilian economy under presidents Jair Bolsonaro and Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva. Sometimes, the Russian operators drafted status reports on the research projects before they had been published, indicating close engagement in the production process. We identified well over 60 articles on the SRC website, the great majority of which appeared to be original compositions. This sets Dark Clark apart from the Russia-origin operation we exposed recently, which also ran a think tank as a front, but plagiarized most of its content; it suggests an intention to build a more durable front entity, with contacts throughout Latin America.

To promote the SRC’s work, the Russian operators claimed that they had tried to create a network of social media assets. According to their reports, this social media activity ran into problems when operators in different parts of the world tried to access the same accounts, leading to account restrictions. Traces of this can be seen in the open-source record: for example, the transparency settings of the main SRC account on X showed a German location (possibly as a result of VPN use) but a connection via the Russian Federation App Store, while the transparency settings of one of its Instagram accounts showed an admin location in Venezuela. None of the three SRC-branded Facebook accounts that we identified had enough followers to trigger admin location settings.

According to recent reports, occasionally, as well as using AI to update reports, the operators did ask our model to help design and create texts that they could use in their operations: for example, asking for help in making sure that their fakes used the correct accent, vocabulary, and institutional style for different countries. This was a relatively small proportion of the overall workload, perhaps because the operation appears to have included at least one native Spanish speaker from Latin America, and therefore had less need of AI language support.

As such, their own claims of impact in their internal reporting cannot be taken at face value. Similarly, some of the fake stories that they claimed to have planted did not show up in open-source research, potentially because they were spread in non-public channels, already taken down, or not actually posted.

According to recent reports, however, open-source evidence allowed us to corroborate some of the claims that they made. Some of the fakes that the operation claimed to have spread were subsequently reported by fact checkers and debunked by governments, suggesting that they had spread widely enough to merit a response. In other cases, we were able to identify content online that closely matched the content generated by this operation. Most notably, the operation’s claim to have tricked a Peruvian school into holding an event that portrayed Bandera was subsequently reported on in both Latin America and Europe, and appears to have led to comment from at least one Polish MEP.

Iran-origin operation seeding long-form articles under deceptive bylines in online news outlets.

In a recent development, we banned a cluster of ChatGPT accounts that originated in Iran. They prompted in Persian, and generated content in Persian and English. The actors obscured their location by using VPNs to access ChatGPT.

The operation used our models to refine long-form articles and editorial pitches, generate social media comments, and refine internal reports. The operators used seven different bylines to pitch their articles, so we have dubbed their campaign “Bogus Bylines.” The long-form articles focused on the US-Iran conflict. The majority of the comments likewise dealt with the conflict; a minority dealt with US politics, typically in the wider context of the conflict.

The apparent impact of their main workstreams varied significantly. We identified almost 100 articles published or syndicated under the operation’s bylines across roughly a dozen online outlets around the world. These were small to medium outlets, generally focused on international affairs, geopolitics, and events in the Middle East. By contrast, none of the social media comments appears to have generated substantial engagement in terms of likes, shares, or further replies. In fact, the operators’ internal reports used a deceptive calculation to measure their social media impact, possibly in an attempt to inflate the operators’ apparent effectiveness.

Sources indicate that the overall activity appeared consistent with a commercial actor running a for-hire influence campaign, but we are unable to identify the particular actor involved. We have shared information on this case with the relevant authorities.

This operation’s most far-reaching activity consisted of asking ChatGPT to review and refine long-form, English-language articles about the geopolitics of the US-Iran conflict. Typical prompts asked the model to assess an English-language draft against the submission criteria for a specific online outlet, and suggest edits to make sure it complied. Once the operators approved the edits, they asked the model to generate an email that they could use to pitch the article to the editor of the online outlet in question.

According to recent reports, our latest model advances bring stronger capabilities for building and migrating software in low-resource programming languages such as COBOL, Fortran, and Visual Basic. These languages have less code and documentation available for models to learn from than languages like Python and JavaScript, yet support critical infrastructure and essential services around the world. COBOL supports⁠(opens in a latest window) more than 40% of online banking systems, 80% of in-person credit card transactions and 95% of ATM transactions, while Fortran is used in weather forecasting⁠(opens in a new window) and nuclear reactor safety analysis⁠(opens in a new window). Improving AI’s ability to work in these languages could help teams maintain and modernize the software that essential services depend on.

This work is guided by feedback from developers and enterprise customers who have told us that coding models often struggle with these languages. Maintaining and modernizing this software can be difficult: documentation may be incomplete, original developers may have left the manufacturer, and notable business rules may only exist in code. Whether teams are fixing bugs or migrating to another language, they need to preserve the behavior their systems depend on.

To measure progress, we developed two evaluations. MultiLangBench tests whether a model can build a SQLite-compatible database engine from scratch across 20 different programming languages. Our COBOL migration evaluation tests whether a model can migrate existing programs to another language while preserving their behavior. Together, they test two complementary skills: building new software and preserving what existing software does.

Our latest models show substantial improvements across both evaluations, delivering much higher accuracy at lower cost.

Industry observers note that on MultiLangBench, GPT‑6 Astra achieves an average test pass rate of 87.4% across 20 programming languages, up from 26.8% for GPT‑5.6 Sol, with improvements in every language tested. GPT‑6 Luna also outperforms GPT‑5.6 Sol, reaching 40.3% at approximately 78% lower estimated API cost.

On our COBOL migration evaluation, GPT‑6.1 Sol achieves 86.6% accuracy at low reasoning effort, compared with just 5.4% for GPT‑5.6 Sol, while costing 39% less. At higher reasoning effort, accuracy reaches 97.9%, nearly saturating the evaluation.

Together, these results show that our models are becoming significantly better at working with a wide range of programming languages, while also becoming more cost-efficient.

MultiLangBench asks a model to build a SQLite-compatible database engine in a specified programming language. The model must organize a codebase, manage state, handle errors, and add fresh features while preserving existing functionality.

According to recent reports, to compare implementations across languages, we use database files as a shared interface. The candidate program receives an initial database and a SQL query, then produces a modified database. We use the reference SQLite implementation to check whether the resulting database has the expected contents. This lets us apply the same tests regardless of the language used to build the engine.

It has been revealed that we adapted tests from SQLite’s existing test suite to this format. The evaluation measures how much working functionality a model can build over an extended coding session, reporting both test pass rate and API cost.

Our second evaluation asks models to migrate COBOL programs to C++, Java, Kotlin, Python, or Rust while preserving what each program does.

A migration is considered successful only if all tests pass. Behavior-preserving capabilities like these are useful for teams maintaining and modernizing older systems, though applying it to production software still requires expert review and testing against the system’s business rules and dependencies.
