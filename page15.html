Today’s updates are designed to encourage and empower researchers, and to continue building a collaborative, community-first security environment.

Recent findings suggest that to build long-term relationships and continuously empower our security community, we are introducing a tiered loyalty bonus program. This 5-tier system provides scaling bonuses to recognize, support, and celebrate researchers who consistently partner with us in making AI technology safer for everyone.

In a recent development, to support community engagement as technology evolves, priority campaigns will feature dedicated bonus pools for newly launched features. These short-term campaigns give researchers early, high-reward opportunities to test new developments and be recognized for their early contributions.

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Launching a national program to help our future compute campuses support reliable, affordable electricity.

Industry observers note that today we’re launching the Grid Protectors Program, a national effort to support affordable, reliable electricity for families and businesses in the communities where we build. Working with utilities, regulators and policymakers, we’ll develop arrangements for our future campuses to reduce electricity demand when the grid is under strain.

Whether it’s heating homes during a northern cold snap or running air conditioning through a southern heat wave, families need reliable electricity. Our goal is to make campus flexibility a resource that utilities can call on when communities need it most.

With U.S. data center capacity projected to exceed 100 gigawatts by 2030⁠(opens in a fresh window), we have a once-in-a-generation opportunity to strengthen the electric grid by building campuses that can adjust their power use when communities need it most.

According to recent reports, through Grid Protectors, our ambition is to make this kind of flexibility a core part of how we build and operate future campuses. That means designing facilities to reduce electricity use when needed and working with utilities to agree on how much demand can be reduced, how promptly and for how long. The arrangements will differ by location, and we will share additional commitments as they are finalized.

Depending on local needs and the equipment available, we will also work with grid operators to supply power from on-site generation or storage, and use batteries and power controls to smooth changes in demand.

Sources indicate that power lines, substations and other grid infrastructure builds are a fixed cost whether demand is low or high. By using more electricity when the grid has room and reducing our use when it is under strain, we can help pay for that infrastructure while limiting the need for costly upgrades to meet peak demand. That can help keep costs down for other customers.

Industry observers note that america needs more electricity to support economic growth, and AI is becoming a significant part of that demand. Families and small businesses should not be asked to subsidize a compute campus’s electricity needs. We’ve always been committed to paying our own energy costs, including the electricity we use and the infrastructure needed to serve our campuses. Large computing facilities should pay their costs and help utilities manage the systems they depend on.

For our planned Effingham County campus, we have committed to pay the full infrastructure and electric-service costs required to serve the project. Once the campus is operating, we can reduce the power we draw during times of need, helping preserve electricity for other customers.

It has been revealed that our agreement with Georgia Power also includes⁠(opens in a latest window) up to 1,000 megawatts of flexible demand response, freeing up power to help keep air conditioning running in hundreds of thousands of homes during extreme heat. For scale, that is equivalent to the average electricity demand of roughly 700,000 households. That’s more households than Washington, D.C., and Boston combined.

These compute facilities are much different than data centers built even a few years ago, allowing us to be flexible in our power demand.

This means we can turn down our demand so that families, small businesses, and other ratepayers will continue to have power. Building grid assets is expensive and Georgia Power notes this flexibility can reduce the amount of upcoming generation it needs to build to serve future growth. Although the campus is still in development, we intend for Effingham to serve as a pilot for Grid Protectors, building on an agreement that already provides a model for future campuses.

Sources indicate that imagine a restaurant in a vacation town. It needs enough tables and kitchen space for the busy season, but it has to pay for that capacity year-round. Regular customers help keep it in use and cover the bills when tourism slows. We are proposing to be that regular customer.

Electricity demand also rises and falls with the seasons, with different regions facing their biggest peaks at different times. Our compute campuses need power year-round. With the right agreements, that steady demand can help spread the grid’s fixed costs across more electricity sales, while flexibility lets us reduce demand when the system is busiest. Building agentic-scale source control while investing in the Git ecosystem.

By Aaron Friel, Taylor Blau, and Ted Nyman, Members of Technical Staff.

Sources indicate that over the past year, the size and demands on our monorepo have increased at a staggering pace. It was clear that we needed to revamp our source control system to scale to the level of concurrent work that Codex makes possible.

We built Pando, a fresh backend for Git repositories, without interrupting any engineering work or changing how our team uses Git. Pando now powers all development in OpenAI’s monorepo. Pando is designed to meet two ambitious goals: first, horizontally scalable writes, optimized for concurrent pushes to many branches. And second, maximize read throughput, so clients can download data fast enough to fully saturate their available network bandwidth.

We met these goals while retaining compatibility with the Git protocol and client, enabling both our engineers and agents to seamlessly adopt Pando into their existing workflows. In production, Pando cut median push latency by nearly 5x. It now delivers nearly a petabyte of Git data per day.

In a recent development, here, we’ll cover our motivations for building a newly introduced source-control system, along with why we chose to retain compatibility with Git. We’ll compare our system with common approaches to scaling Git, describing why Pando is uniquely capable of meeting OpenAI’s scalability needs. We also discuss what’s next, and how we’ll continue contributing to Git to support agent-scale work.

Codex has changed the scale and pace of development at OpenAI, enabling engineers to do far more work in parallel. That means more branches and pushes, more merges, and more fetches and clones from development environments, research jobs, and CI.

Industry observers note that these demands compound. One engineer now has several changes ready for CI at once, and each push can set several jobs fetching the same commit.

Starting around the end of last year, the systems supporting our monorepo were struggling to keep up. We suffered slow fetches and pushes along with failed merges, with reliability at times falling below 90%. Had we reached a limit of Git itself, or of the way we served our monorepo? Git grew out of a community that exchanged patches to the kernel via email. For more than two decades, developers have adapted their tools around Git as the way they build software has changed. When we started to feel friction in our own monorepo, it mattered to us that we could keep building on that work.

We saw a way to keep that familiar workflow while rebuilding the backend to handle pushes to different branches in parallel and serve the same history to many readers concurrently. Building on Git as the foundation of our source-control workflow meant that improvements to the client could be useful beyond OpenAI. We wanted to contribute those improvements from our copy of Git⁠(opens in a latest window) to upstream, sharing them with the broader community.

Sources indicate that scaling a source control system has two essential dimensions: many writers pushing to different branches, and many readers fetching the same history. Pando optimizes for both.

First, Pando coordinates updates to each branch independently. Updates to unrelated branches can proceed in parallel, and a reader fetching a branch gets the history needed for that revision. The tradeoff is that a reader looking at several branches can see them at different points of time.

According to recent reports, second, Pando reuses packfiles (Git’s format for compressing multiple objects together) across fetches instead of generating an individual packfile for each client. A client may download some objects it already has, but the server can serve the same bytes to many clients with little computation.

Recent findings suggest that these choices let us scale writes across branches and serve reads from caches. To see how they fit together, let’s start with how Git stores and transfers history.

Git must be able to keep two parts of a repository in sync: objects and references.

It has been revealed that A Git repository records snapshots of files and the history connecting them as a graph of objects. An object may be either a blob, tree, commit, or tag. Blobs hold the content of an individual file, and trees associate names with blobs or other trees to form a directory structure. Commits point to a root tree and zero or more parent commits, forming the history of your repository.

It has been revealed that objects are identified by a hash of their type and contents. Objects are also immutable: recording a changed file creates a new blob, while unchanged files and directories can reuse existing objects.

According to recent reports, branches and tags are references, or “refs”, that point to commits. Committing on a branch creates a new commit and updates the branch’s ref to point to it.

In a recent development, git objects reference each other by their ID, commits to trees, and trees to trees and blobs. The branch refs/heads/main points to commit C2, whose parent is C1. C1 points to root tree T1, where parser.py refers to blob B1. C2 points to root tree T2, where parser.py refers to blob B2. In both root trees, docs/ refers to the same tree, T3. Its README.md entry points to blob B3, and usage.md points to blob B4.

Recent findings suggest that git stores objects in either a “loose” or “packed” state. Loose objects are stored in individual files identified by their object ID. Packed objects are stored together in a packfile, enabling similar objects to be stored as deltas against one another.

Git can store blobs as separate files or pack them together, encoding one blob as a delta against another.

Recent findings suggest that in Git, an object is reachable from a commit if Git can find it by following links from that commit through its parents, trees, and blobs. During a fetch, the client tells the server which commits it wants and which it already has. The server finds the objects reachable from the wanted commits, excludes those it knows the client already has, and sends the remainder as a packfile in the same format Git uses on disk. A full clone starts without any objects, so it needs all the objects reachable from the history being cloned.

A push transfers objects in the other direction by asking the server to revision one or more refs. The client uses the server’s set of advertised refs to avoid sending objects the server already has. The pack being sent can therefore depend on existing objects: receiving the pack alone is not enough to reconstruct the newly introduced history.

Replicating a Git repository means keeping both objects and references available across multiple servers. Before a replica can serve a reference’s newly introduced value, it must be able to supply the objects reachable from that value. The service also needs to coordinate competing reference updates, i.e., pushes that try to move the same branch from the same old tip to different new commits. Systems that impose a single order on all writes to a repository can make pushes to unrelated branches wait on one another.

While designing Pando, we evaluated conventional approaches for scaling source control using Git: one that coordinates ref updates across repository replicas using three-phase commit (3PC, a protocol for coordinating a commit across multiple nodes), and one that orders updates through a shared write-ahead log (WAL, a record of changes written before they’re applied) in blob storage.

Recent findings suggest that in the three-phase commit design we modeled, the Git service keeps a complete copy of the repository on several replicas. When any server receives a push, the incoming packfile is copied and sent to all replicas in parallel. To publish the push, a majority of replicas must accept the ref revision. The server managing this is the coordinator, and it requires multiple rounds of communication between the coordinator and each other replica.

To accept a reference patch, each replica locks its copy of the ref and checks that it still points to the commit the client expects. The coordinator then sends a pre-commit message to the replicas that accept. Once a majority acknowledges the pre-commit message, the coordinator tells them to perform the update. Any up-to-date replica can then advertise the newly introduced branch tip from its local copy of the repository.

Keeping complete replicas makes it possible to serve reads locally, and guarantees that clients get a consistent view of all references no matter which replica they pick. As a consequence, references are updated one at a time, and each push requires multiple round trips to coordinate the ref revision among replicas.

Recent findings suggest that waiting for a quorum puts replica disk performance and communication latency on the push path. As write volume grows, the replicas must keep up with incoming packs as well as read traffic.

In the WAL-based design we modeled, the Git service uses a shared manifest in blob storage to order updates across the repository. When a server receives a push, it uploads the incoming packfile to a blob store and prepares the ref upgrade in its local copy of the repository. To publish the push, it adds an entry to a shared manifest stored in the blob store that records the ref update and points to the packfile. The Git server then updates the manifest using a compare-and-swap operation. This atomically replaces the manifest, or otherwise rejects the change. If another writer has updated it in the meantime, the server reads the intervening entries, checks that its ref update is still valid, and retries using the packfile it already uploaded.

It has been revealed that writers can upload their packfiles in parallel, but even pushes to unrelated branches require updating the same manifest. The round-trip time for a successful compare-and-swap limits how often the repository can publish a new manifest. A low-latency blob store makes this step fast. Batching lets one manifest upgrade several refs at the same time, at the cost of queueing updates and increasing latency.

It has been revealed that the blob store can receive packfiles in parallel, but updates to the shared manifest still compete with one another. Increasing the blob store’s capacity alone doesn’t resolve that bottleneck. Each replica still needs to read and index packs from the blob store before it can serve them locally. If incoming writes outpace that work, replicas fall behind and clients wait longer to fetch newly pushed commits.

It has been revealed that that brings us to Pando. Pando is a fresh architecture for serving Git repositories with the goal of scaling to many clients pushing simultaneously. Here’s how it works:.

Prior to developing Pando, we had a hunch that we could meet our requirements without compromising on Git, but we did need to compromise on some of the things that most Git systems assume to be requirements:.

Industry observers note that repository-wide consistency: Providing a consistent, point-in-time snapshot of all refs requires coordination across the repository. In a system that orders all ref updates together, pushes to unrelated branches contend on a single coordination point.

Recent findings suggest that unique packfiles for every client: If two clients with different local histories fetch the same branch, the server may need to generate a different pack for each to avoid sending objects they already have. Creating these responses costs CPU and disk I/O, making it difficult to scale to high volumes of readers.

Instead, Pando provides a relaxed consistency model (causal consistency) to scale writes, and reuses packfiles to scale reads.

In a recent development, when a client pushes to a branch, Pando stores the incoming packfile as an immutable blob in a blob store. Each time the branch moves, Pando records a ref version containing the new tip, the pack that supplied it, and any earlier ref versions the pack depends on in a metadata store. Both the blob store and metadata store are horizontally scalable.

It has been revealed that fetching a commit or reference from Pando requires replaying a set of ref versions (packfiles) to the client, reconstructing the state of the client that created the ref version being fetched. This provides a weaker form of consistency across the entire repository, while providing serializability for updates to individual refs. Clients only conflict when pushing to the same ref simultaneously. A client reading several branches cannot assume those reads describe a single point in time across the repository.

Sources indicate that A push to trunk adds an incremental pack, containing one or more commits. Each push to a side branch includes the topic’s earlier commits again, so a client with the advertised trunk version can fetch the topic in one pack.

Industry observers note that this is a form of causal consistency. In order to fetch a reference, the reader observes the history of the writer that produced it. During a push, Pando controls what shared history the client and server agree on, ensuring clients produce more easily shared packfiles.

Industry observers note that as a consequence, Pando doesn’t provide a point-in-time state of all references during Git operations or an ordering of ref updates within a clone or fetch. Concretely, suppose that branches foo and bar are both updated during the negotiation of a full clone. During the negotiation, one client may observe foo at version 1 and bar at version 2, while another client observes foo at version 2 and bar at version 1. In practice, customers and agents are agnostic to the global ordering of ref updates, and instead depend on the order of updates to a single reference.

It has been revealed that on handling a push, Pando validates the incoming packfile while streaming it to blob storage, without unpacking it into a local repository. When a client begins a fetch Pando selects the stored packs the reader needs, and the reader downloads them. No server needs to apply packfiles to a local repository or perform costly unpack and packing operations. Because these servers primarily perform negotiation and packfile selection, Pando servers can run on commodity hardware.

According to recent reports, this allows Pando to scale to the limits of clients, the network between clients and Pando, and the blob storage service. With enough capacity, clients working on independent branches can each transfer packs as fast as their own connection allows. To serve more clients at that rate, we can add Pando servers and scale the metadata store and blob store alongside them.

Pando stores the writer’s packfile in blob storage and records the ref version in metadata service. On fetch, Pando selects the packs the reader needs, and Express serves them.

In a recent development, an agent edits files and creates a commit locally and runs git push, which creates a packfile of those changes to patch a branch. Pando receives the pack and streams it to blob storage. After validating the incoming push with a single pass decode, Pando uses a multi-key transaction to record a ref version in our metadata store and atomically update the ref pointer. This enables Pando to provide “read-your-own-write” consistency. This entire write operation requires O(1)O(1)O(1) metadata reads and writes.

Sources indicate that when another client fetches the branch, Pando selects a set of stored packs using the history the client already has. This operation requires O(log⁡Δ)O(\log \Delta)O(logΔ) metadata reads, Δ\DeltaΔ being the shortest distance in ref versions between what the client has and what the client wants. The server returns packfile URIs, and the client downloads them through a service, Express, that stores packfiles in regional caches.

This design relies on the three central services of Pando’s architecture: pushes, fetches, and compaction.

Recent findings suggest that when a client pushes, a conventional Git server is like a poker player showing all of its cards, revealing to the client every branch it has. The client then gathers the commits, trees, and blobs to create a packfile that excludes objects it knows the server already has.

According to recent reports, advertising more refs can make the push smaller. It can also make the pack harder to reuse, because another client may be missing objects the writer left out.

Pando takes a different approach. Instead of showing its full hand, it only advertises a select set of branches to the client. This is done for two reasons:.

Industry observers note that providing a consistent view of every ref is expensive. With millions of branches, the ref advertisement alone can exceed 100 MiB. Providing this requires each server to either maintain a current mapping, requiring significant coordination overhead, or a database query that scales with the number of refs.

By providing only a subset of the refs to the client, the client will generate a pack that depends only on what the server explicitly allows the client to exclude. Tracking the dependencies allows Pando to reuse the packfile a client generates in one push by serving it as the response to a subsequent fetch.

As a Git server advertises more refs, clients pushing have more sources from which to exclude objects, enabling them to produce smaller packs. However, reusing these packfiles requires the server to be able to prove the clients fetching have each of the dependencies. Otherwise, the packfile may omit objects the client needs.

Recent findings suggest that on the other hand, if a Git server advertises none at all, the client would generate a packfile containing the repository’s full history. That packfile can be used by any other client, but at the significant cost of making fetching any branch effectively a full clone.

According to recent reports, advertising more refs results in smaller packfiles. Most of the size reduction can come from advertising just one.

By default, when a client pushes a new feature branch, Pando advertises only a designated trunk branch as a base. This keeps the advertisement the same size as the repository gains branches, but comes with tradeoffs. When an engineer either pushes to a single branch many times, or stacks one feature branch on top of another the client has to resend everything outside of the trunk.

According to recent reports, this causes successive branch pushes to be larger, containing potentially-redundant objects, and uses additional client bandwidth. In our monorepo, the median packfile produced this way was under 20 KiB, and the 99th percentile was under 3 MiB. For us, this was a sensible trade-off because a reader that already has the advertised trunk history can fetch the branch with that one packfile.

Sources indicate that after choosing the set of refs to advertise to the client, Pando accepts the push and updates its metadata store. Pando splits the metadata update into synchronous and asynchronous portions. The synchronous metadata update ensures clients can immediately fetch the newly updated commit. This writes a newly introduced ref version and updates the ref pointer mapping to that new version storing that new tip, the dependencies used to negotiate the packfile, and a link to the packfile that was pushed.

At the same time, asynchronous work is recorded in the same transaction, such as scheduling compaction. For very large pushes, only a subset of commits are written synchronously. This allows us to require only a constant number of reads and writes to the metadata store, regardless of the size of the push.

Importantly, Pando servers can accept pushes to unrelated refs independently. The only contended key is the ref pointer, which we guard using an atomic compare-and-swap-like operation. We can scale up write throughput by adding Pando servers, metadata store scaling units, or blob stores.

Industry observers note that when a client performs a fetch, it provides the server with two sets of commit IDs, known in the protocol as haves and wants. In a conventional Git server, the server computes the set difference among the objects reachable by the haves and those reachable by the wants, compresses them, and sends the result to the client. While the open-source Git server has a variety of indexes locally to accelerate this operation, these packfiles can be expensive to compute and are generally resistant to caching.

In a recent development, instead, Pando uses the commit mappings recorded on pushes to look up the client’s haves and wants, resolving them to a set of ref versions that contain those commits. Each of those ref versions links to a packfile previously received by Pando. The server uses this to solve for what the client needs, selecting the packfiles that cover the set of objects the client needs.

Pando compares what a client has with what it wants, then selects the stored packfiles needed—including any missing branch dependencies.

Select a branch to include its trunk dependencies.

Pando maps the Git protocol’s haves and wants to a sequence of ref versions. Wanting a side branch implies wanting up to its dependencies on the trunk.

The Git protocol only allows a single packfile to be sent in the HTTP response on a clone or fetch. However, an under-appreciated feature of the Git protocol, packfile-uri, allows a server to return links to download additional packs separately. Pando uses this exclusively, and transmits an empty packfile inline. To support this feature, OpenAI contributed a number of improvements to packfile-uri upstream.

OpenAI systems operate in many geographic regions, so we maintain Pando clusters with local caches in each. A pack delivery service, Express, caches our blob store, and a separate service caches the metadata store. These caches enable Pando to serve requests for a commit ID or a special read of a ref entirely from the cached state in that cluster. We use consistent hashing to route requests for a packfile to Express servers, with cache hit rates of over 99%.

This system of caches provides extremely high throughput, capable of saturating the network limit between clients and Pando. Using these packfile URIs and local caches, we’ve validated that using only standard cloud provider virtual machines, we see clone and fetch operations saturate 10 Gbps network links.

For most Git servers, repacking and garbage collection are necessary to maintain the performance of a growing repository. Both of these steps reclaim space, but are compute- and I/O-intensive.

In a fast-moving repository like OpenAI’s monorepo, it is infeasible to send an individual packfile URI for every push that has occurred since the last fetch. Each day, a client would need to download tens of thousands of packfiles to catch up their trunk.

Instead, Pando combines packs in the background to reduce this overhead, adopting a design similar to compaction in log-structured merge-trees. Each push produces what Pando refers to as an “L0” packfile, representing the smallest unit of data Pando can transmit. Over time, background workers collect these packfiles into covers spanning a sequence of ref versions, scaling geometrically from L0. Today, our monorepo uses up to L4 packfile covers.

Industry observers note that these covers serve a dual purpose. After a cover is sufficiently full, a compaction worker will claim it and produce a single packfile. This improves the compression ratio by reorganizing the objects inside the packfile. But it also improves fetch negotiation performance by acting as skip list over ref versions. By scanning through indexes at each level simultaneously, Pando can select a set of packs to send to a client in logarithmic time. These packfile covers, repacked or not, enable a more efficient solution to fetch planning, reducing both throughput and latency requirements for Pando.

Small packfiles are grouped into larger covers across levels, improving compression and speeding up fetches.

Compaction combines packfiles from many ref versions in the background, enabling more efficient fetches with fewer files.

Unlike many Git servers, packfiles are kept at every level and not discarded or garbage collected. Packs at every level are used to serve clients continuously, based on the haves and wants they request in a fetch. And for clients using Pando as an API, reading an arbitrary object from a packfile tends to require reading less data from an L0 than an L4, because it’s less likely to be compressed.

Sources indicate that compaction in Pando significantly reduces both the total transfer size sent to clients and enables pack planning to scale sub-linearly in how far behind the client is. This is especially key for large and promptly changing repositories, which we expect to be a new normal in software development.

According to recent reports, we built Pando with Codex, moving from kick-off to production, in just 10 weeks. The core of Pando was designed by a single engineer, implemented by teams of agents, and deployed by a handful of engineers. Pando began serving production traffic in April of this year, and is one part of a larger system of source control management tools used at OpenAI.

We employed a wide variety of approaches when validating Pando’s design. We applied our principles for building more secure software foundations⁠. As a greenfield project, we chose to implement Pando in Rust and without legacy dependencies. We wrote a conformance test suite so agents could test their changes against standard Git behavior. In a recent development, our initial deployment of Pando was as a read- and write-through proxy to our existing source control system. This allowed us to keep Pando and the previous system in sync, so we could safely move traffic onto Pando without downtime.

To the best of our knowledge, the agents in this pipeline discovered and used at least 10 novel Linux exploitation techniques.

The Linux maintainers and security community are working hard to rapidly discover and fix vulnerabilities. Within 10 days while the scan was running, we observed over 100 Linux LPEs had been patched or had staging patches from other contributors, with ~300 more being publicly discussed.

Industry observers note that given the rate of vulnerability discovery by frontier models, we believe automated patch generation is essential to secure software at the pace required, and to limit the exploitation window. With human expert-guided iteration, we believe AI-generated patches can be functionally correct, aligned with existing coding styles, and robust to attack variants. This is an important area for further research.

More capable models are able to discover upcoming vulnerabilities in existing code, often including ones that have been around longer and are more complex to identify. Our analysis pipeline using the newer cyber model in mid-October discovered >5x more novel LPEs than a GPT‑5.6 Sol run in July, despite three months of hardening between the scans.

Large, complex software projects likely require multiple rounds of scanning and fixing vulnerabilities to “bottom out” latent bugs. Ideally this process is repeated with each subsequent generation of frontier models. This applies to both proprietary and open source software.

For the near and medium term, having access to a low-privileged account on an operating system, that isn’t up-to-date on its latest available patches should be considered functionally equivalent to root access for a well-resourced threat actor. Defense in depth, using sandboxes and isolation approaches like Firecracker and gVisor are vital for limiting the blast radius of exploited vulnerabilities.

Within the next few years, we believe that if there are vulnerabilities in a given software project, frontier models will likely be able to discover them and even less capable models may be able to develop exploits once disclosed. In a world of no unknown vulnerabilities, it will be critical to build and maintain high assurance software, especially core software such as operating systems, browsers, image parsing libraries, the TCP/IP stack, and more. Investing in training models to write secure code by default, minimizing attack surface by removing unnecessary features, and formal verification are promising areas of future research to effectively scale software security, in addition to finding and fixing existing vulnerabilities.

Industry observers note that the Linux kernel underpins Android devices, cloud infrastructure, supercomputers, and other systems used by billions of people. With more than 40 million lines of code, the kernel exposes a broad attack surface through system calls, networking, filesystems, and device drivers. We chose Linux to study how effectively frontier models could find vulnerabilities and demonstrate their security impact in a codebase of this scale and importance.

The initial vulnerability discovery stage produced thousands of candidate findings, too many for security researchers to manually review and triage. The operational challenge of our vulnerability discovery pipeline was not discovery, but determining which findings could be reproduced, what security boundary they crossed, and which warranted the earliest maintainer attention.

The mission of OpenAI’s Cyber team is to help secure the software the world relies on. We report API costs for each stage to help researchers assess the resources needed to reproduce and extend this work. These costs reflect the models, target configurations, and workflow used in this evaluation. Our broader goal is to help defenders find and fix vulnerabilities, reducing the opportunities available to attackers and raising the cost for them to exploit software.

Finally, note that we focused on methodology and measuring model capability in this research, not cost. We expect that sufficient harness engineering could meaningfully reduce the cost at each stage; thus, the costs we report are more an upper bound.

Our vulnerability research prototype is a pipeline of five stages, which uses an upcoming cyber model at its core:.

Vulnerability discovery: Agents identify security-relevant parts of the codebase and scan them for candidate findings.

Dynamic validation: Agents attempt to reproduce each candidate in an isolated environment and preserve runtime evidence. This step outputs the reproduced and validated vulnerabilities.

Exploitability assessment: Agents test whether validated vulnerabilities could enable information disclosure or local privilege escalation.

Patch generation: Agents generate patches that address each identified vulnerability.

Human review: Security researchers review the exploit, patch, and proposed severity before reporting to the maintainers.

At the end of the pipeline, the system bundles a root cause analysis, a minimal crashing proof-of-concept, a candidate patch, and a full exploit to assist human researchers in assessing the severity of the vulnerability and upstreaming the fixes.

In a recent development, not every line of code in the Linux kernel is security-relevant. Our pipeline used a team of Scout agents to identify high-value audit targets, which were then passed to Analyst agents for vulnerability scanning.

Scout agents read through each folder and file and determine what targets to audit based on their reachability (reachable by low-privileged consumers) and security relevance.

It has been revealed that this stage identified 5,034 audit targets, about 2.8 million lines of code, which is roughly 7% of the whole Linux kernel code base, significantly reducing the cost of the security audit when compared to a full codebase analysis. This step cost $3,426 in API credits.

Sources indicate that next, analyst agents analyzed each audit target and produced candidate findings using a workflow similar to that of the Codex Security plugin⁠(opens in a fresh window). At a high level, the analyst agents read the assigned audit target’s code to understand how it works, identify its threat model, and look for vulnerabilities in it.

Recent findings suggest that within 12 hours, the analyst agents produced 2,308 candidate findings after deduplication. This stage cost $316,549 in API credits.

A candidate finding is not the same as a validated vulnerability. At this scale, manually investigating every report is impractical for our research team.

We therefore focused on validating findings that a) were reachable by a low-privileged user, b) required no special capabilities, and c) produced measurable evidence of memory corruption.

For each candidate, a triage agent generated a proof of concept and ran it in an isolated virtual machine running a Linux kernel with Kernel Address Sanitizer (KASAN). The agent observed both the program output and system console, then iteratively refined the proof of concept until it reproduced the reported behavior or exhausted the potential ways of triggering it.

Industry observers note that within 30 hours, our system reproduced 1724 vulnerabilities, ~74.7% of the 2,308 candidate findings. Static code review alone can be prone to false positives, so we were impressed by the accuracy of the analyst agents and the effectiveness of the triage agents in autonomously creating working proofs of concept for almost three quarters of the candidate findings.

Sources indicate that most remaining findings either required elevated privileges or failed to reproduce within the time budget because of difficult race conditions, specific trigger conditions (e.g., a required allocation failure at a specific line), or time-consuming validation (e.g., refcount wraparound). Only 3.1% reflected incorrect analysis by the model, where the reported vulnerability assumed an impossible condition.

Recent findings suggest that the validation stage cost $73,330 in API credits in total. We observed that in our validation pipeline, which needed to generate a proof-of-concept to confirm if a candidate is a real vulnerability, this process was more expensive than making a recommendation based only on static analysis, and the costs of attempting to validate a true positive and false positive were similar. Thus removing false positives earlier in the pipeline makes the process meaningfully more cost-efficient.

Sources indicate that validation establishes that a vulnerability is real, but not how urgently it should be addressed. A vulnerability that could enable local privilege escalation, for example, warrants greater priority than one that could only cause a local denial of service. With 1,724 validated vulnerabilities, the next task was to identify the findings with the greatest potential security impact.

Recent findings suggest that an initial screening agent reviewed each report, crash log, and relevant source code statically. It was deliberately permissive to avoid discarding potentially serious findings, while filtering out cases without a plausible exploitation path. This narrowed the results to 313 information leak candidates and 1,238 local privilege escalation candidates.

Exploit-development (ExploitDev) agents then tested those candidates in isolated virtual machines configured with the protections enabled by a standard Linux distribution, including Supervisor Mode Execution Prevention (SMEP), Supervisor Mode Access Prevention (SMAP), Kernel Address Space Layout Randomization (KASLR), and HARDENED_USERCOPY. This helped prevent unrealistic assessments that depended on protections being disabled.

The agents produced 291 proofs of concept for information leaks and 1,169 working local privilege escalation exploits that could give unprivileged local owners root access.

We performed variant analysis and developed 948 distinct patches for the vulnerabilities behind those exploits. We use that number to determine the overall number of unique LPEs. Successful exploits cost an average of $359 in credits to develop. That makes it relatively inexpensive for defenders to test the exploitability of found vulnerabilities.

The initial vulnerability screening agent cost $44,194 in API credits. The exploitation stage cost $775,929 in API credits in total, $84,929 for processing information leak candidates and $691,000 for local privilege escalation candidates.

On average, processing a local-privilege escalation candidate ($558) was roughly two times more expensive than processing an information leak candidate ($271). This is because information leak PoCs were carried forward from the validation stage and required less modification to become usable in an exploit. On the other hand, developing an exploit from an LPE candidate required many extra steps such as exploitation strategy planning, identifying vulnerable objects to use, mitigation bypasses and more. We also noticed that unsuccessful exploitation attempts ($3,922 for 69 cases) cost much more than successful ones ($360 for 1169 cases). This was caused by a few hard-to-exploit vulnerabilities (e.g. race conditions). Defining a reasonable give-up condition, such as stopping the attempt after making no progress for one hour, would have reduced the cost of ExploitDev significantly.

For each validated vulnerability, the system also generates a patch. We provide the model with the relevant source code and prompt it with best practices in patch writing, such as performing static checks on the patch, following the software development practices used in nearby code, and performing variant analysis to make sure the patch is complete.

Following the Linux kernel community’s suggestion, we have already sent out hundreds of reports and patches for critical vulnerabilities and are working with Patch the Planet partners to gradually send out patches for the rest. This work is part of our broader Daybreak commitment to support open-source projects.

In this section we share learnings, reflections, and additional analysis from this research.

During this research, we observed the Linux maintainers moving quickly to fix vulnerabilities and protect people. The scan was performed on the latest Linux kernel in mid-October 2026. When the autonomous scan and manual analysis finished after 10 days, 61 LPEs had already been patched upstream, 43 had staging patches, and 299 were being reported and discussed publicly.

In a recent development, we greatly appreciate the Linux maintainers who have been working urgently to patch identified concerns, and the broader security community contributing their findings as well. This collective response is what’s needed to secure the world’s software. The rapid rate of vulnerability disclosure and patching also emphasizes the criticality for owners and companies to keep software up to date.

It has been revealed that takeaway: Automated triage should take the rapid patching cycle into account. Revalidating findings against the latest code, including upcoming code that was merged after the original scan, helps avoid duplicate or obsolete reports. This focuses attention on unresolved vulnerabilities and gives maintainers evidence that they can act on without adding unnecessary review burden.

While we were developing the pipeline, in late July 2026, GPT‑5.6 Sol found and exploited 166 unique LPE vulnerabilities for an end-to-end $513,690 cost in API credits.

In a recent development, in the second scan, in mid-October 2026, the newer cyber model found and exploited 948 unique LPE vulnerabilities for an end-to-end $1,213,430 cost in API credits. For both campaigns, we scanned the latest Linux kernel master branch at the time.

Takeaway: Rescan previously audited code as model capabilities improve. A more capable model may uncover vulnerabilities that earlier models missed, even when using the same research harness.

According to recent reports, we sampled 166 exploits developed by the agents and analyzed the exploitation techniques used in them. To the best of our knowledge, the agents used 10 newly introduced exploitation techniques in the sampled exploits. We shared the exploitation techniques with third-party kernel exploitation experts and the techniques were new to them as well.

In a recent development, of the 10 techniques, two are new persistent elastic objects that the model used to spray objects inside the target kernel cache; two are new page grooming techniques to improve exploit reliability; five are new data-only techniques to obtain privilege escalation directly or via an arbitrary write; the other technique involves arbitrary type confusion. For safety reasons, we omit sharing the full technical details of these new exploitation techniques in this post.

Takeaway: Frontier models do not just pattern match known vulnerable code patterns or solely use known exploitation techniques; they demonstrate creativity and novel approaches.

An important part of our team’s mission is to measure the cost of finding and exploiting vulnerabilities and use that evidence to guide defensive investment. Our goal is to help defenders fix vulnerabilities before attackers can use them, and make it cost prohibitive for attackers to exploit software. Measuring exploitation costs helps us assess how that challenge changes as model capabilities improve. In this section, we compare our pipeline run’s cost with bug bounty program rewards for the vulnerabilities identified.

Industry observers note that the target virtual machines in our experiment ran a stock desktop operating system, not a weakened target, under the same threat model that is used by cybersecurity competitions (e.g. Pwn2Own⁠(opens in a latest window), TyphoonPWN⁠(opens in a new window)). Historically, Pwn2Own and TyphoonPWN offered bug bounties of $20,000 and $70,000, respectively for Linux kernel local privilege escalation exploits. Applying those historical bounty amounts to the 948 exploits produces a hypothetical valuation of $18.9M—$66.3M, approximately 16x to 55x our pipeline’s cost.

In a recent development, we also tested our pipeline against Google’s kernelCTF⁠(opens in a upcoming window) vulnerability reward program, which features a hardened Linux kernel with reduced attack surfaces as the target. We ran the pipeline on the latest version of the Linux kernel (incompatible with the SLAB_VIRTUAL mitigation in kernelCTF so we dropped it from the config) under kernelCTF’s environment and obtained 25 information leak proofs of concept and 21 local privilege escalation exploits. For comparison, 26 confirmed 0-day LPE exploits have been accepted by kernelCTF in 2026 at the time of writing. The whole campaign lasted 112 hours in total, costing roughly $32,936 in API credits, with vulnerability discovery accounting for 83.7% of the API cost. If the 21 vulnerabilities were accepted by the program, it would lead to around a $1.5M bounty award, approximately 45x the cost.

According to recent reports, takeaway: Based on public pay-out amounts, with current cyber-capable models, our vulnerability research pipeline was net profitable at the time of this research. Defenders should proactively analyze their software so that it becomes cost prohibitive for attackers to exploit.

During exploitability assessment, ExploitDev agents were given a vulnerability report and tasked with creating a fully functional exploit. Our pipeline marked a vulnerability as exploited if a root shell was successfully obtained. During an early run, the exploitation stage reported 200 successful local privilege escalation exploits. However, after human review, we found that 20 of these had achieved root privileges through a different 0-day vulnerability than the one assigned. This suggests that even successful exploitation by agents needs proper validation.

We subsequently tightened the task constraints and added an independent check confirming that each final exploit used its assigned vulnerability.

Takeaway: For autonomous security research, verify the path to a result in addition to verifying the result itself; isolation, monitoring, and independent checks are necessary controls.

Sources indicate that early in this research, we explored the design decision of whether to build heavy orchestration around the agent to improve its cyber capability. Our result suggested that stronger models require lighter orchestration and hand-holding. And the marginal improvement does not justify the maintenance cost. As a result, we decided to remove the heavy orchestration.

The experiment compared GPT‑5.5 Cyber and GPT‑5.6 Cyber. In our observation, GPT‑5.5 Cyber did not consistently identify when and how to apply existing Linux kernel exploitation techniques. We then assembled 103 techniques in a model-friendly format, made them available in the agent’s environment, and prompted the agent to use them. Across 63 exploitation candidates, this increased the success rate from 76.2% to 88.9%.

According to recent reports, when we repeated this with GPT‑5.6 Cyber, the model was better able to find, select, and apply relevant techniques on its own. We removed the technique/skill orchestration and this lightly orchestrated GPT‑5.6 Cyber workflow outperformed the more heavily scaffolded GPT‑5.5 Cyber workflow. Adding back the additional technique layer for GPT‑5.6 Cyber provided only a marginal 1.5 percentage-point improvement.

Sources indicate that takeaway: Reevaluate your orchestration and harness as base model capabilities change. Scaffolding that materially improves one model generation may add little value for a stronger model and should be evaluated for the maintenance tradeoff.

Initially, when directly prompted to generate candidate patches, the model would create functionally correct patches that mitigate the proof of concept but occasionally introduce other issues: more code than necessary, introduced potential performance tradeoffs, deviated from coding conventions, or failed to examine related code for variants of the same vulnerability, leading to incomplete patches.

Additional instructions and iterations on the patch generation process improved the quality of patches, which suggests that with proper handling, it is possible for AI-generated security patches to be correct, minimal, and consistent with existing code conventions. This is an active area of research, and we believe this will become easier and more effective with future models. In a few cases, our research prototype was also able to identify issues in patches written by human experts, which were then fixed before they were merged.

Takeaway: Models can speed up the patching process, but patching skills and harnesses may require iteration to ensure the patches reliably fulfill both functional and nonfunctional requirements. Additionally, we recommend using models for adversarial review of human and AI-generated patches, which can identify problems such as the patch being bypassable, missing other attack paths or variants, and more.

Industry observers note that in our kernelCTF run, besides the 21 Linux kernel 0-day exploits, our research prototype also found and exploited a QEMU TCG vulnerability. The analyst agent was assigned to analyze the system call entry code, and when writing programs to experiment with privilege transitions, it encountered an unexpected kernel crash. It triaged the crash and traced the concern to QEMU TCG, without access to QEMU’s source code, and included the vulnerability in the report. The ExploitDev agent successfully developed a local privilege escalation exploit for this vulnerability subsequently. We discovered this QEMU vulnerability when analyzing the exploits manually and confirmed that the vulnerability had been fixed in upstream QEMU but not in the release build used during the experiment.

According to recent reports, takeaway: Frontier cyber-capable models can find vulnerabilities in testing infrastructure. We recommend testing each part of the infrastructure before carrying out offensive vulnerability research. We have proactively tested our infrastructure, disclosing and patching many vulnerabilities in Kata, Moby/Docker, gVisor, and other projects.

Our goal is to turn increasing model capability into a lasting advantage for the people securing the Internet. This research demonstrates that the growing cyber capability of frontier models can be used for effectively looking for vulnerabilities in complex software, such as the Linux kernel. Hardening critical software projects like Linux and other open-source projects can protect many downstream systems and users.

In a recent development, through Patch the Planet, a Daybreak initiative, we aim to extend this research to more critical software projects and develop reliable automated patching alongside maintainers and security partners. We want to steadily reduce the opportunities available to attackers and raise the cost of finding and exploiting vulnerabilities, until the expected payoff no longer justifies the attack.

Every generation of models should help us build software that the next generation finds harder to compromise. We will continue working towards building a secure Internet that anyone can joyfully build on.
